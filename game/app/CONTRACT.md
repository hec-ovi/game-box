# @gb/app contract

contractVersion: 0.31.0

## Purpose

The game you can play: the landing screen your cities are laid out on, the panel you make a new one in, the blueprint of the architecture it lays out before anything is written into it, the pack that grows one of them, the loader while the model writes one, the renderer, the frame loop that streams the city round the player, the first-person body, the locks it opens, the screens it sits at and what is playing on them, the counters it buys at, the car it gets into and the one a job paid out, the train between stations, the grade that turns the hour into a look, and the wiring that turns every other box into a city you walk around and an interface that reads it.

## Inputs

| Param | Schema | Preconditions |
|---|---|---|
| the landing screen | one wide card per city on the shelf, newest first | what the player lands on when the address bar names no city; picking one is what starts a game |
| the panel | theme, what the city is about (unbounded), the main quest, the side quests, the tone, the style (`neon`, `density`, `wear`, each one of `@gb/world`'s levels), blocks across and down, the doors that open, the tallest building, the seed | on screen with the first byte of the page; every field optional, blank meaning the generator chooses; `blocks` is held to what the generator will found, 1 to `@gb/forge`'s `BLOCKS_MAX`, and `storeys` to 1 to `@gb/world`'s `TALLEST_STOREYS`, blank being `@gb/forge`'s own ceiling, which is high enough for a skyline |
| a field of the brief written by the model | which of the five (`theme`, what the city is about, the main quest, the side quests, the tone), and everything typed so far | Generate with AI beside that field, or the one in the action grid that asks for all five; only with the sidecar answering |
| a draft of the brief | the form as it stands | Save draft, on the first step. Kept in this browser and nowhere else, and read back into the form the next time the panel is opened |
| an architecture to look at | the same brief | Generate the city, on the first step: `@gb/forge`'s `Forge.plan`, which is the town with nothing written into it |

| a pack file | a `.gbpack.json` picked off the player's own machine | cut from the city that is open. A pack names the city it was cut from, so one cut from another is a sentence on the panel |
| the player's own settings | the address of a video for the televisions | kept in this browser and handed to the game; never part of a brief and never written into a city |
| which AI runs which job | the providers the AI service holds, and the five jobs pointed at them | the same two groups on both settings screens: the one behind Settings on the landing rail, and the settings tab in game. The service holds all of it, so whichever screen writes, the other reads it back |
| a provider's key | typed into the key field of an external provider | write only: it goes to the service on the same tick and is never drawn back, never kept in the browser and never in a push |
| the shelf | a card per city the player made or opened, newest first, the last one marked: its name, what it is about, its size and seed, when it was written, whether a playthrough is waiting in it, and Continue or Open beside Remove | the library is read when the panel is built and again after every change |
| URL query | `?bundle=` a world file, or `?seed=`, `?theme=`, `?blocks=`, `?places=`, `?storeys=`, `?sidecar=` | a city named in it is opened straight away, which is how a city is shared; with none of them the landing screen waits for a pick, and with an empty shelf the form does. `?sidecar=` is written back untouched, so a refresh keeps it |

Left out, each is the real one |
| `Boot.start(query)` | a `URLSearchParams` | |
| `Game.start(mount, bundle, options)` | an opened `@gb/bundle`, `GameOptions` | the bundle opened, so its world and quests are sound |
| `GameOptions` | `{ dressing, room?, cast?, kit?, cars?, sidecar?, ai?, save?, leave?, stage?, screens? }` | `ai` is the hand on which AI runs which job, shared with the launcher; without one the settings tab draws none of it. `save` is where the playthrough is kept between visits; `room` dresses one interior at a time and is handed the interior's charter; `leave` is what leaving means, decided by whoever started the game; `stage` is where the frames are drawn, `createStage` unless the caller has no GPU to give it; `screens` is the address of a video for the televisions |

## Errors (closed set)

None at the boundary. A city that will not build and a file that will not open are sentences on the panel, not throws: the player reads what went wrong and tries something else, and a file whose places are of a kind it does not describe is refused by those words. A missing asset pack is a duller city, not a blank screen. A browser that will not open its database keeps nothing and says so in the console; the game still starts.

## Invariants

- This box holds no rules. Quests advance in `@gb/quest`, inventory changes in `@gb/play`, conversations happen in `@gb/talk`, geometry is built in `@gb/scene`, a city is written in `@gb/forge`. Everything here is wiring, input and frames.
- The panel is served in `index.html`, so it is on screen with the first byte of the page rather than after the renderer, the art and the city have loaded. It says what is happening at every step of that wait, and offers a way to stop it.
- **Every field on the form is optional, and blank is absent.** A blank field goes to the generator as no field at all, never as an empty string, so a player who wants a city presses Generate and gets a good one. What the city is about is the player's own words and has no cap; the main quest, the side quests and the tone are lines for the writers; the style is three choices inside `@gb/world`'s levels and nothing else, because the art is one catalogue and a period or a look outside it cannot be drawn. The form says so beside the choices rather than taking a word and dropping it. The answers go into the world file through `@gb/forge`, so a shared city says what it was asked for.
- **A field of the brief is written by the model or it is not written.** Beside each of the five fields the model can write (the theme, what the city is about, the main quest, the side quests, the tone) is a Generate with AI button that asks `@gb/scribe`'s `writeBrief` for that one field, handing it everything already typed; the one in the first step's action grid asks for all five. It is the same button in the same words with the same icon wherever it sits, because it is the same thing every time. The answer goes straight into the box and every other box is left exactly as it was. While the call is out the button says it is generating and no other takes a press, and the box it will fill is untouched until the answer lands. Pressing one asks for that field alone, and the build asks for the whole town. With no model reachable the button says so and changes nothing: there is no canned text behind it, because canned text handed over as the model's answer is the thing this replaces. The call goes through the page's one `Sidecar`, so a busy model is the same wait it is everywhere else, and it is pinned to a seed of its own each press rather than to the city's, or asking twice would hand back the same words.
- **A control answers where it was pressed.** What one button could not do is a plain line on the reserved slot inside its own group (`[data-notes]` round the control, `[data-note]` for the line), not a red sentence across the panel's foot: the foot says what the panel as a whole is doing, and a message that sends the player to another step to fix a button on this one is not an answer. The line holds its height whether or not it has words in it, so nothing under it moves.
- **The first step lays a city out before it writes one.** Generate the city asks `@gb/forge`'s `Forge.plan` for the architecture on the press: the grid, the roads, the named parts of town, every building with its height and its part, and the stations, with no interiors, nobody in them, nothing lying about and no quests. No model is asked anything, nothing is filed on the shelf and nothing is played; it is fast enough to run under the button rather than behind the loader. Preview blueprint opens it and is out of reach until there is one, plainly disabled rather than dead. Moving any control drops it, because a layout is of the settings it was laid from. Save draft keeps the form in this browser and builds nothing, and the panel opens on that draft next time, ahead of the city the player was last in.
- **The blueprint is the architecture and nothing else.** It draws what the plan holds: the roadway and the pavement, every building as a box at its own footprint and height, the parts of town as the shapes their blocks make with their names floating over them, and a mark on every station. There is no sun, no weather, no hour, no rubbish, nobody walking and nothing to walk into, because a plan has none of that and the view adds none. It is not the game: the camera turns round a point on the ground and never stands on it.
- **The blueprint is drawn rather than dressed.** `@gb/scene`'s `buildCity` is the game's city: ground textures, marked streets, rubbish, lights, shells and rooms, and a dressing to draw them with. A blueprint wants none of it, so this box draws the massing itself and takes from `@gb/scene` the one rule it must agree with, `storeyHeight`, so a tower in the blueprint is the tower the game builds. `@gb/kitbash` is not asked for anything: it draws buildings that look like buildings, which is the opposite of what a blueprint is for.
- **A part of town is the shape its blocks make, derived once.** `@gb/hud` publishes `districtShape`, so the interface and this box read one derivation: the cells the district covers, every cell edge facing a cell it does not, the runs along one line joined, and the name at the middle of its largest block. Each block is carried out to the middle of the street round it (`HALF_STREET` in `blueprint/plan.ts`), because the blocks of one district never touch and a shape derived off the blocks alone is a heap of outlined blocks rather than a part of town. Reading one holds its colour and steps every other one back. Nothing about a part of town stands up off the ground: it is a wash, a line round it and its name, because a plan marks a zone on the ground.
- **The map in game is the blueprint, and the seam is one canvas and one port.** `@gb/hud` renders what it is pushed and owns no renderer, so it holds a glass out (`hud.mapSurface`) and this box draws the city into it, moves the camera and answers what a thing picked off it is (`read` in, `reading` back). Both surfaces of that drawing come from `src/blueprint/`: `plan.ts`, `cells.ts` and `orbit.ts` are plain maths with tests over them, `massing.ts` and `pins.ts` are the three.js of it. The camera is direct manipulation: the ground follows the pointer on both axes, and dragging turns the town the way it is dragged.
- **Nothing steps while the map is up, and the drawing costs a frame only when something moves it.** A window open pauses the game, which skips the scene's draw altogether, and the city on the glass has no clock and nobody in it, so it is drawn where the input arrives and never again. Measured in Chrome on this machine's WebGL2 fallback, an 8 block city at 1920 by 943: **0.4 ms of main thread a frame with the map open (p90 0.5) against 6.6 ms with the city running (p90 16.2)**, on a 100 Hz display. The city is built on the first open and kept, so opening it again is one draw.
- **Opening the blueprint costs the launcher nothing until it is pressed.** The view and three.js are fetched on the press (`Show`, `boot/boot.ts`), and the tile says it is opening while they arrive. Measured in the browser on a 20 by 20 city (2895 buildings, 7 zones, a 551 by 543 grid): **344 ms** from the press to the first frame on the glass counting the fetch, and 182 to 269 ms to build and draw it again after that. Opening it neither builds, files, shelves nor starts anything, and leaving hands back the form with every field as it was typed.
- **The blueprint draws when something is done to it and not otherwise.** Nothing in a plan moves, so there is no loop: the pointer, the wheel, the zone list and a resize each draw one frame where they happen. The first frame is drawn before the view answers, so the panel is told it is open once it is on the glass and never waits on a frame a hidden tab will not give it.
- **What the form says about the city is what the form was told.** The readouts on all three steps are one `[data-said]` slot each, filled from the brief the fields hold: the grid, the seed, how many instances open, how tall it may build, the look, and the four lines of writing, each saying "the generator chooses" where the player left it blank. The zones are the one readout that is not a field, and they say so: `@gb/forge`'s `districtCount` is the rule and an upper bound on a town nobody has cut yet, so the slot reads "About 6" until a layout has been laid and the town's own `world.districts()` replaces it. How many buildings go up, who lives in them, how many cars are on the road and how the quests run are settled while the city is built, and the generator cannot be asked before it runs, so the form does not say them at all.
- **The last step asks once, in sentences, out of the brief alone.** Build reads the whole brief back as plain lines and only then generates, so what the player confirms is what `@gb/forge` is handed.
- **A city somebody sent is opened by choosing the file.** The panel takes the `.gbworld.json` Export wrote, with no conversion and nothing to edit in an address bar, and a file that is not a city is a sentence on the panel rather than a throw. A file written before charters opens, and the player is told it reads against the presets it was drawn with.
- **Leaving takes the city off the screen.** The way out hands the keys back, takes the mount off the screen so no part of the town is drawn behind the front door, and asks the game to stand still (`Playing.pause`); coming back puts the mount on again and sets it going. The game is kept rather than disposed, because Continue has to be instant and a dispose would build the town again; opening a different city disposes the one that was up, which is where a game actually ends. Nothing steps while the panel is up: no clock, no crowd, no traffic, nothing streamed. The renderer's loop turns until `Stage` publishes a stop, so what a city off the screen still costs is one draw of a scene that never changes (handover 271).
- **The front door is the cities the player already has.** With no city named in the address bar the panel opens on the landing screen: every city on the shelf as a wide card of its own, newest first, the one they were last in marked, each saying its name, what it is about in their own words, how big it is and how it was asked for, when it was written and whether a playthrough is waiting in it. Nothing is generated and nothing is entered on its own, because a bare address that dropped somebody into a town they did not choose is the game choosing for them. A shelf with nothing on it opens on the form instead: a grid with nothing in it is a dead end on a first run. A city named in the address bar, by file or by the seed and theme that build it, still goes straight in, because that is how a city is shared and how a refresh comes back to the one being played. The two faces are the same card and only one of them is on the page at a time; the way out of a game lands on the landing screen with the keyboard on the city it came from.
- **Every city is on the shelf, with its playthrough.** A city made here is filed under the brief it was made from (`keyOf`, a digest of everything the generator reads), so asking for the same city again replaces the document and keeps the save; one that came in as a file is filed under its own hash. The save is keyed the same way, because every generated city calls itself `world_0001` and the key is the one name that tells two apart. The newest entry is the city the player was last in; opening one moves it to the top; removing one takes its save with it. The documents live in the browser's database, since a city file runs to megabytes.
- **A refresh comes back to the same city rather than writing it again.** The shelf holds the document, so nothing is written again: an address naming the brief the last city was made from opens that city, an address naming another brief generates it, and a bare address opens the last one. A city that was written again under the same brief resumes the save reconciled, and `@gb/bundle`'s report is said on the screen by name: what was kept, what was dropped.
- **The loader is the hud's, driven by the scribe.** `@gb/hud`'s loading view goes up on its own interface for the wait, every progress event of `@gb/scribe` redraws it (`history`, `city`, `places`, `quests`, each a bar), and it is taken down before the game's interface is built, so two interfaces are never on the page at once. The panel stands aside for it: the form folds away and what is left is the step and Cancel along the foot.
- **A busy model is a wait, never a failure.** The sidecar is built here with `onBusy`, so every wait it sits through is announced as `model-busy` with the seconds on whatever interface is up, the loader or the game. Nothing here retries anything: the box that owns the call asks again. A call that came back wrong or not at all is one `error` notice for the build, saying how many and why; a kind of place the history asked for and the city would not take is a note with the reason.
- **Where the player is headed is drawn twice off one list.** A goal mark per open step of every live quest, `{ x, y, label, kind: 'goal', line }` in grid cells, goes on the map beside the player's own mark and on the corner view, so a place on the plan and the same place in the corner are recognisably one place. The map carries two more that the corner does not: an `offer` on every door somebody is holding work behind, so a player on no job can read where to start, and a `home` on every place they hold the deed to. The story carries `line: 'main'` outright, because a goal with no line reads as an errand. The interface never reads the city: a mark it is not pushed is a mark nobody can see. A mark on somebody at their post carries the plot of the place they keep, so the plan writes that building's name on itself and a route walks to its door.
- **A job is knowable once the player could walk up and take it.** `QuestLog.offeredBy`, asked of every person in town, is the whole rule: the giver is somebody the city can be asked where to find, and the job's `requires` are already met. Every one of those goes to the interface as `offers` (`src/places.ts`), each carrying the job, whose door it is behind and the building to look in, so the panel writes a name and never a marker id. Measured on a 4 by 4 city built offline (`seed: probe`, 18 people, 19 quests): 11 knowable on the first frame, 1 on the main line and 10 errands, against 0 pages in the journal, which is why the lists read empty until this is pushed. It is measured again whenever the board moves, because taking, finishing or giving up a job is the only thing that changes what is left on offer.
- **Two jobs behind one door are one place to walk to.** The plan gets one `offer` mark per door and the lists get one row per job, both under that door's own handle, so picking either row reads the same building and no two pins land on one spot.
- **A player holding nothing is pointed at the story.** With no open step to follow, the compass and the guide resolve to the door the main line starts behind, so `G`, the strip and the plan all answer where to go and get the first job instead of answering nothing.
- **The corner view is the city windowed here.** The streets round the player, where they stand and face, the goals and the doors of every place they have walked into go to the interface as `minimap`, in the map's own cells, windowed to a radius this box picks. It is measured again when the player crosses a cell, when the quests move or when half a second has passed, and pushed on the tick the compass is pushed, so turning on the spot moves the corner with the strip. Indoors it is `null`: a room has its own metres. Turning it off is the player's setting and takes it off the screen; the game goes on pushing it.
- **The screen is the browser's and the settings tab reads what the game pushed back.** Full screen is a request the browser may refuse and the player may leave with a key of its own, so `settings.fullscreen` is pushed from the document's own `fullscreenchange` and never from the click that asked for it. The corner view's setting is pushed the same way, off what the game holds rather than off the button. Nothing here binds `F`: that key is the interface's.
- **The way out is asked once, and not here.** The interface asks "you sure" in place and reports `exit` only on the yes, so `exit` means hand the game back to the launcher and the panel asks nothing more; the no arrives as `stay` and there is nothing to do with it.
- **The keys belong to whatever is taking what the player types.** The game binds on the document, so a conversation, the panel while it holds the keys, and any text box anywhere on the page are all asked first: naming a city cannot turn the hour over twice on its way in, and a space in a text box is not swallowed as a jump.
- **The panel is in front of everything, so the interface gets out of its way.** Handing the keys over shuts whatever window the hud had open, because a window left standing behind the panel takes Escape and Tab while the player is pressing them at the panel. The way out is the interface's `exit`, from its key, its Leave button and its settings tab; the way back is Escape on the panel, printed on the button.
- The same theme, seed and block count give the same city, in the browser and out of it. Nothing here adds a number of its own to a brief: the block size and the roads out come from the seed, in `@gb/forge`.
- A brief is held inside what the generator will take before it is sent, so a block count nobody could build is trimmed rather than refused, and what remains that the generator still turns down comes back as a sentence.
- **A city is written by the model or it is not written.** Every build hands `@gb/scribe` to `@gb/forge`: the town's history, its names, the people in it and the work they hand out all come back from the model, and there is no setting on the form and nothing in the address bar that writes one another way. A call `@gb/scribe` cannot make good stops the build, and the sentence it stopped with is the sentence the panel says, because a line saying that something went wrong throws away the only part the player can act on. That sentence is read off the failure whether it arrives thrown or as the `message` on what `@gb/forge` refuses with.
- One city is being made at a time. Asking for another stops the one in flight, and so does Cancel: a generation runs against one `AbortSignal`, which `@gb/scribe` carries down to the model. A conversation is stopped by walking away from it, which breaks out of the reply stream and releases the call.
- **A city made here is pinned to the art it was designed against.** The catalogue lives in code, so a world file that does not record which building of the pack each plot was given is re-skinned by the next reader whose pack has grown: on the two block city the panel makes by default, growing the catalogue by one look moves **15 of 39** buildings unpinned and **0** pinned. So the art is loaded before the city is sealed rather than after it, the pack is named in the bundle's `requires` and every plot it has a building for carries the model it was given. The size the pin is written against is the size `@gb/scene` hands the dressing, in metres, or the pin names a model of the wrong shape and the reader falls back instead.
- **A pack that would not load pins nothing at all.** A city with no catalogues promises nothing and is honestly unpinned; one naming a catalogue with no plots pinned to it looks pinned and is not. A world that took the catalogue and then refused a design is that second city and cannot be undone, so it comes back as a sentence on the panel rather than a file.
- Export writes the document the game opened, not a fresh pack of the same world, so the file and the playthrough cannot disagree.
- **A finished city is added to in packs.** A pack is what somebody built onto one city later: it names that city by world id and content hash, applies to it and to no other, and gives the same city on every machine. Opening one onto the city that is playing files the result under the row it was already on, so the playthrough carries into it and `@gb/bundle` reconciles whatever moved. Growing is the other half and is the two commands `gb extend` and `gb pack` take: the base is opened twice, one copy is grown by `@gb/forge` and pinned to the art the growth was designed against, the pack is cut from the two, and it is applied straight back before the player is handed it, so a pack that would not apply is never written. Only what went up is pinned; every record the base wrote comes back byte for byte or there is no growth to cut.
- **What plays on a television is the town's own arithmetic, unless the player says otherwise.** `@gb/furnish` computes every set's picture from the clock, which is what a world file can carry: a video in one would be bytes in every copy of that city for ever. A player who wants their own picture gives the panel an address, and it is theirs alone: kept in this browser, handed to the game, never part of a brief and never written into a city. Set and playing, it is what every set in town shows; a source that is blank, unreachable or refused leaves the town's own schedule running and says so in the console. The rectangle it plays in is the glass `@gb/furnish` publishes on the vertices of the set, so a repacked television keeps its picture in the right place with no table here. The city is never held up for it: rooms built after it comes up get it, and the room the player is standing in when it does gets it there and then.
- **A refresh comes back to the same spot, not the same city.** The city is built the same way every time, so what a save has to say over the top of it is put back last: the room the player was behind the door of is opened before their metres are read, because a room is measured in its own metres from its own corner, and a door that will not open for them any more leaves them on the pavement rather than at a room's metres out in the city; whoever was walking with them sets off from beside them rather than from the post the city just put them back on; and the job they were following is followed again, unless it is one nobody is holding any more, which is let go rather than pointing the map at it.

Nothing here remembers a rate or a pause of its own.
- One unit is one metre. Inside the built area the walls come from the grid the city was generated on; past it the land says how high the ground is and whether it can be stood on, so the player walks out of town onto open country rather than into the edge of the map. No physics engine, no baked collision.
- The player is placed on the pavement a step off the first door in town that opens, looking at it. Most buildings are shut, so the first door on the street is usually one nobody can go through, and a player who opens their eyes on a blank wall has nothing to press. The step is taken back off the kerb where the pavement is deep enough and along it where it is not, so the opening frame is a street rather than a facade. Entering a building puts them inside it facing the room.
- **The face on the conversation panel is the person's own.** It is drawn from a second copy of them, spawned into a scene this box keeps for the purpose and lit by two fixed lights, so it is the same face at every hour and on every machine and the one standing in the street is never moved out of the world for a frame. The copy is stepped by no time before the shot, because a body spawns with its clip playing and its skeleton still in the bind pose, which is arms straight out at shoulder height; one step writes the first frame of that clip and they stand like a person. It costs one body build and one render, once per person, and it is kept: walking back up to somebody is the same face with no second build. The camera stands in front of them: a body spawns facing -Z, and standing on +Z is standing behind them. It frames `MARGIN` heads of height with the head at the top, so what is under the face is shoulders, and there is nothing behind them: the panel has a ground of its own to show through. When the copy is done it is only taken out of that scene, never disposed: everything drawable under it is `@gb/cast`'s, shared by every clone of that outfit, so handing any of it back takes the buffers off everybody wearing it out in the city. The codex shows the same faces, reading only the ones already drawn: it lists people the player has talked to, so their face was drawn when they spoke, and opening a panel of names must not start a render.

That is a second renderer, and it is affordable because it only ever draws while the inventory is open, which is exactly when the game behind it is paused: one small canvas, one object, two lights, no shadows. It is made on the first thing opened rather than with the game, so a playthrough that never opens the inventory never makes a context for it, and the thing itself is built once and kept.

A quest picked out of the list beside the city reads as wherever it is sending the player, so a row and a label answer the same way. A thing with no walk to it says so.

It is two clock reads a segment and silent on a good frame, so it stays on in the running game: a stall of a second is a pipeline compiled the first time a material is drawn or a room built on the frame somebody walked at its door, and none of that is in a number a bench can take.

Lane rather than town, because an avenue carries four lanes and the road out four and one flat number per town left the wide roads empty. Who is out is read once on the way through the door, because the street stops while the player is inside.

On a turn their own words carry all of it, which is why it is announced on the way in and only there.

Everybody else is at the post the city gave them.
- **A step that names a subject is credited by the conversation and by nothing here.** `@gb/talk` puts the subject on the menu and sends `talked` with the topic when the move is taken; the app forwards no `talked` of its own, so a chat that wanders onto the subject credits nothing and a step still on the board cannot be finished from outside.

A click comes back as that move's own key and goes straight to the conversation, which carries it out with no model call; typing goes the way it always did. Walking away is left off the menu, because the panel already ends a conversation two ways the player can see. - **Whoever is being talked to comes out of what they were doing to face the player**, and goes back to it when the conversation ends. A pedestrian stops mid-route, comes round, and walks the rest of their route afterwards; a companion stops keeping up and catches up again; somebody at their post in a room is handed to `@gb/cast`'s `attend(point)` on the opening and `resume()` on the close, so a worker bent over a desk straightens up and turns rather than staring over a shoulder, and the spot the room measured for them is never moved. An interior is dressed by its own art, so the body of somebody indoors is that room's to hand over and no other set has it; asking the city's dressing alone finds nobody and leaves every counter in town holding its stance. The point is handed over again as the player walks (every quarter metre, not every frame, because each is a turn to ease into). Without the art pack a body at a post turns only as far as its head cannot reach, so a shopkeeper never swings their back to their own counter. It happens on the conversation opening, not on being looked at: a pedestrian who stopped every time the crosshair crossed them would bring the pavement to a halt.
- **Who walks with the player is `@gb/play`'s list, and the bodies follow the list.** A click on somebody in reach, a conversation in which they agree to come along (`@gb/talk`'s `follow_player`) and a job that puts them beside the player (`companion-join`) all write that list and nothing else; whenever the board moves, whoever is on it and not yet walking sets off and whoever is walking and no longer on it goes back to their post, so no way of agreeing leaves a flag with no body behind it. Somebody in the player's car counts as walking.
- **A companion comes in through the door and goes back out through it.** `@gb/scene` says where a visitor may stand in a room and `@gb/crowd` stands them there on the body they walked in with, the first of them on the first spot the room offers and the next on the second; talked to in there, they are held and turned by the same call the pavement uses. Coming out puts them back on the doorstep. Somebody standing in a room is never measured against a doorstep in the city's own metres, because a room is measured in its own metres from its own corner.
- **An escort is credited when the person walking with the player gets there.** `@gb/quest` takes one event for it, `companion-arrived { npcId, place }`, and it is sent when that companion's body comes within four metres of the doorstep of the building the step names, or when the player goes through its door with them; once per arrival, until they have walked off again. Agreeing to come is not arriving.
- **A locked door is a wall with a way through it.** The file says which doors are locked and what opens them; the playthrough says what the player carries, was given and owns. A door opens for the key or card in hand (the thing's own `opens`, or the door naming it as its key), for access a job granted, for the word the player was given, and for whoever owns the place, whatever its lock. Opening one gives `@gb/nav` its edge back, so the route and the map walk through it, and tells the quest log the lock came off (`unlocked`). A lock that has come off stays off for the rest of the playthrough, and one the player opened themselves is written down as access, so it is off again when the city is built back from its file. What the playthrough can already get past goes back into `@gb/nav` before the first route is asked for, because the city is built again from its file every time with every lock back on.
- **A gate of bars is the lock you can see.** A `bars-door` is not one of the room's own blockers: it stands across the door it names for as long as that door is locked, at the door's own cell and to the size `@gb/world` gives it, and it is walked through the moment the lock comes off, with no room rebuilt.
- **The screen at a desk is `@gb/hud`'s, and what it runs is the city's.** Walking up to a terminal, laptop, tablet or monitor and pressing the act key opens the screen off `Furniture.machine`: a ledger lists what that building really has standing on its surfaces and what it is worth, the mail is a line from everybody stationed there in the words the city gave them, a camera feed names the room each of that building's cameras watches and who is standing in it, and a machine running nothing says so. A locked screen opens to its own word, typed at it, which credits the hack (`machine-unlocked`); a word a job already handed out opens it on sight, because a word the player was given is one they have read. The games are the hud's to play: the score reported when one ends is kept per machine per game in `@gb/play` and pushed back as the best without restarting the board, and the quest log hears the run (`scored`).
- **A counter is opened by the person keeping it.** `@gb/talk`'s `show_wares` is somebody naming their stock, and that is what puts the counter on screen: what they sell is everything of theirs standing on a surface in the building they keep, at the price the city priced it at, less anything the player already carries or has moved. Buying is `@gb/play`'s `buy`, reported twice, `bought` and `acquired`, so a job that says to buy it and a job that says to fetch it both see it; the counter goes back out after a sale without the thing sold on it, because the interface takes nothing off it itself, and it is taken down with the conversation it was opened from, because it is that person's counter and not the room's.
- **A deed is the house it names.** Buying one, or being handed one by a job, writes the owner into the city (`recordOwner`) and opens that place's street door for its owner. The city is built again from its file every time, so the places the playthrough owns are written back into it before anything asks whose they are.
- **A car a job paid out stands at the kerb outside the player's own door.** `@gb/drive` only ever takes a car off the road, so the one the player was given is put on the road here: drawn from the pool the traffic draws from, offered on the same feed and handed over the same way, so getting into it is getting into any other car. Parked, it is solid to walk into and something the traffic brakes for, and the playthrough remembers which car is out, so it is standing there again on the next visit.
- **A thing is put down where a job asked for it, and it is standing there afterwards.** A `stash` step names the room and the surface, and the room was built with a spot standing at that surface, so leaving something is walking up to it and pressing the same key that picks things up. `@gb/scene` draws it on that surface with the rule it built the room's own things with, so it can be seen where it was left and picked back up. The spot is only offered while the job is asking and the thing is in the player's hands, and it is worked out again at the moment the key goes down, so a prompt that has gone stale does nothing rather than something else.
- **A room is dressed as this playthrough left it, not as the city file wrote it.** The file says where everything started and `@gb/scene` builds every room from it, so a thing in the player's pocket would be drawn on its shelf as well and a thing they left on a strongbox would be back where it came from. Both are one thing twice, and either copy could be picked up. What the player is carrying is taken off the shelf, and what they moved is drawn where they moved it, once, when the room is built.
- **The graphics are one port, and the rest of the game runs without them.** `Stage` is a camera, a scene, a canvas and a frame loop; the renderer behind it is named in one file and reaches nothing else, so `Game.start` handed a stage of its own builds the whole city, the crowd, the traffic, the interface and the input with no GPU anywhere. That is what makes the constructor testable, and the constructor is where the ordering lives: the playthrough is built before the first push to the interface, because that push writes a save.
- **A picture read back off the GPU is laid out the way the API that handed it over lays it out.** `Stage.snapshot` is the only read of a render target, and the two APIs disagree twice: WebGL reads a framebuffer from the bottom left with its rows packed tight, WebGPU copies a texture from the top left with every row padded up to 256 bytes (at 288 across, 1280 bytes carrying 1152 of picture). Neither validates what the caller assumes, so the wrong layout is a full buffer of real bytes in the wrong places and a face shredded into stripes with no error anywhere. Which API is really under the renderer is read off it (`three` picks the fallback itself), the rows are moved with that layout in `src/readback.ts`, and a buffer short of a whole picture comes back as nothing rather than as noise. The target is sRGB, because a picture handed straight to an `<img>` has to leave the GPU in the space a screen shows: left in linear light it reads back at about a third of the brightness it was drawn at.
- **Whoever is speaking visibly talks, on the pavement as well as behind a counter.** A line is opened on their body when the words start arriving, beaten to on every piece of the line as it comes, and closed when the turn ends: `@gb/cast` lays the talk that suits the stance they hold over their upper body and beats their head to the pieces, so somebody leaning on their counter is still leaning on it and a line that stalls goes still while the hands keep going. It is mimicry, not lip sync, and there is no mouth in the pack to shape. `@gb/crowd` and `@gb/cast` answer the same question in the same shape, so there is one lookup for the whole game, asked in order: the street first, because somebody out walking is not also standing behind their own counter, then the room the player is standing in, then the city's own dressing. It is asked again every time and never kept, because a retired walker's body is handed to the next person out and a member held from one turn to the next is a stranger's arms.
- **A yes and a no are on the body, and neither is nothing.** `@gb/talk` publishes how a reply came down only on the turns that have an answer, so a nod goes over the speaker for a yes and a slow shake of the head for a no, and a turn that is only talk gets neither. Both clips are layers, like the talk itself, so somebody leaning on their counter answers without leaving the counter. The turn's `does` goes to the same layer read for its words: a direction that says they nod or shake their head plays as that, and any other plays nothing, because the model writes prose and never names a clip.
- **Walking off ends the conversation, on the pavement and behind a counter alike.** On the street `@gb/crowd`'s `held` going false is the one signal, whether the player walked past `talkRadius` or the walker was retired off the far end of the street; indoors the crowd has no body, so the same rule is measured here with the crowd's `Leash` and the same radius against the post. The panel closes rather than staying open on nobody. Each conversation is opened afresh on the person in reach: measured through the headless game, a second conversation names the second person on the panel and moves the second body, with nothing of the first carried over.
- **Somebody asked along sets off from where they are standing.** Their own spot on the pavement, read off the crowd, or the doorstep of the building they were stationed in (`crowd.follow({ npc, door })`); never the player's, or whoever they called to across the street appears beside them instead of walking over.
- **The player can drive.** Any car on the road can be taken: `E` on a car within reach gets in, and `E` behind the wheel gets out. The companions ride, and are back on the pavement beside the car when the player gets out. While driving, the first person body is ridden rather than walked: the eye is put where the seat is every frame and the view turns with the car, so the mouse still looks around inside one that is cornering.

- **A person in the road is a person's width, not a body's.** What stops the player walking into somebody is a collision capsule a third of a metre across; what a car has to give way to is wider, and sending the capsule let a car pass half a metre from somebody's middle without slowing. Measured through the headless game on the two block city: ten seconds along the pavement into town re-hands three of the sixteen lights and leaves the nearest of them on the doorstep beside the player, 3.0 m off and within a metre of the door. Into town, because the spawn is a step off the first door that opens and can sit on any edge of the grid: walked the other way the player is out of the built area inside ten seconds, with no doorstep left to hand a light to.

The dressing the city is built with stands aside for the room art of the one interior being built, so the inside of a building is still dressed for that building. That art hands over three things and this box carries all three: the dressing that paints the room, the bays its walls are made of, and the fixtures they burn, which go to the room's own light.
- **A wrapper carries what it does not answer.** Every dressing this box puts in front of another (the guard, the composition that takes a building's far look off the building layer, the seam a room's art is aimed through) names what it speaks for and carries the whole of the rest over from the dressing behind it, whatever the rest turns out to be. The seam grows: the far look, the light off a building, road paint, rubbish and the bodies the cast spawned each arrived after these wrappers were written, and both `@gb/scene` and this box read an answer by asking whether it is there, so one left off a wrapper by hand disappears with nothing on screen to say so. `src/seam.ts` is the one place that carries them, and the next capability added to a seam reaches the game with no wrapper edited.
- **What a building looks like from far off and what it throws onto the street are the building dressing's own answers**, and the chain carries them: the furniture layer speaks for rooms and the cast layer for bodies, so the city takes those two off the building layer directly and the guard passes them through. Dropped anywhere on the way, `@gb/scene` dresses every building in town whole at open and the city has no lights in it at all. Measured on a 20 by 20 town (2,612 plots), below.
- The landscape brings its own sky and light. Plain daylight only comes out if the landscape fails to build, so a scene is never unlit.
- **The sky does two jobs and they are split.** `Land.skyLight` is the light the sky throws, written by the landscape for the theme and the hour (2.20 at noon, 0.78 at midnight on the temperate theme), and it is what keeps a face the sun never reaches from going flat. The prefiltered dome in `scene.environment` is what a surface gives back, and it is turned down to `Look.environment` (0.06 outdoors) because at full strength it is the brighter of the two by an order of magnitude: a cast shadow takes 1.4% of the light off what it falls on with the dome over the hemisphere, against 39% with the hemisphere alone. Measured on the frame, at 0.06 with both: **a shadow takes 52% of the light off the road it falls on** (sunlit 108, shadowed 52 in sRGB), **two faces of one corner building read 62 and 33**, and sky, pavement and roadway land at 175, 88 and 108 rather than within three points of each other.
- **The light moves every frame; only the prefilter waits.** `land.setTime(clock.secondsOfDay / 3600)` is called every frame, indoors as well, with the fractional hour: `clock.hour` and `clock.minute` are whole numbers, and an hour built from them only moves once a game minute, four times a second at the default rate, so the sun hopped two thirds of its own width a kick and the gradient, the fog, the lamps and the grade with it. Measured through the headless game over two game hours at the loop's longest step (3,000 frames): the sun moved on every one of them, the prefilter ran nine times, and a frame cost 0.034 ms median, 0.120 ms at the 99th and 1.7 ms at worst. The traffic's lamps are given the same fractional hour.
- **The reflection is moved between rebuilds rather than remade, and the rebuild follows the sky rather than the hour.** Prefiltering costs about 20 ms against a 2.5 ms frame, into the same render target every time so `scene.environment` stays one texture object and no shader is rebuilt. Between rebuilds it is carried off `land.light`: `scene.environmentRotation.y` is `sunYaw` minus the yaw it was filtered at, and `scene.environmentIntensity` is `Look.environment` times `skyBrightness` over the brightness it was filtered at, both for nothing, because the sky's pattern is very nearly rigid about the vertical. That ride is only good while it is a correction: measured on the neon theme the dome brightens **fortyfold between 07:00 and 08:00** and falls to a fiftieth between 16:00 and 17:00, and forty times a starfield is not a dawn sky — held to the hour it is what turned one shopfront murky olive and then near-white with nothing else changed. So another copy is taken when the sky has moved past `DRIFT` (1.35) either way, as well as on the hour and on a change of weather. Measured in the running game across 07:00 to 08:00, `scene.environmentIntensity` stays inside 0.046 to 0.080; the extra copies are nine over the two climbing hours from 08:00, and none at all over the flat middle of the day. The dome is put back at the origin for the prefilter, because it rides on the player and the camera that filters it does not.
- The landscape is built for the hour the playthrough is at. The environment is prefiltered off its skydome before the first frame, so a city opened at midnight and built at the landscape's own default midday would stand under a black sky lit like noon until the hour turned.
- Nothing reaches the screen unfiltered. The scene renders into a half float target and stays in linear light through the glow and the colour, and the tone map is the last thing that happens, so a halo rolls off instead of clipping to a flat disc.
- **How dark it is, is one reading, taken in one place.** `1 - land.light.day` is it: the sun's own crossing on the theme's latitude, full night seven degrees under the horizon and full day eleven above. The grade, the wet street and every point light the city hangs read that one number, so the exposure, the lamps and the reflection cannot disagree. It beats `land.light.dark`, which is the stars' ramp and runs about an hour late — printed on the temperate theme at 16:30, `dark` is 0.002 while the sun has already set. There is nothing to step on at an hour edge, and a city with no landscape round it ramps an hour and a half either side of `@gb/play`'s `SUNRISE_HOUR` and `SUNSET_HOUR`. `@gb/kitbash`'s sign and window emissive and `@gb/traffic`'s headlamps still run their own hour curves; at 17:30 that is 1.000 here against 0.000 and 0.500 there, and closing it needs a level setter on those two boxes.
- **Night is the look the city is built for.** After dark the sun is gone and the dome with it, so what a lamp and a sign throw is nearly the whole budget: the exposure comes down to hold the dark, the glow comes on, and the shadows go cold so a saturated hue has something to be saturated against. What is left of the sky is the moon and `Land.skyLight`'s lifted blue ambient, which is what reaches a wall no lamp is on. It is small — measured at midnight on a facade with nothing lit on it, sRGB 30 with the ambient and 30 without, because the night ambient the landscape publishes (0.78 at a colour of linear luminance 0.05) is under a hundredth of noon's. The wall reads dark with its structure showing rather than black, and it reads that way because of the exposure, not because of the ambient.
- **The day has its own look.** The sun is low all day and the dome a five to one gradient, so the exposure is set to hold a sunlit facade and a shaded one in the same frame, with no glow and no tint, because a halo over a sunlit wall lands on the wall and daylight is the colour the art was painted. Measured at noon on the neon theme, one frame carries sunlit road at 108 and shadowed road at 52, pavement at 88, sky at 175 and a shaded facade at 33, with nothing blown.
- What makes neon read is the step from the sign to the dark beside it, not the size of the glow. The threshold sits above a lit wall and under a sign so the letters are what crosses it, and the halo is tight so none of them swallows the letters it belongs to. A wide halo at a high strength reads as fog on the lens: measured on one shopfront at midnight, going from threshold 0.6 / strength 0.6 / radius 0.2 to 0.9 / 0.35 / 0.15 takes the band across the sign from 5.3% of its pixels over 200 to 3.4%, the door lamps from 5.5% to 3.9% with nothing blown at all, and the whole frame from 1.09% over 200 to 0.68% and 0.03% blown.
- The glow is taken off the finished frame, not off an emissive-only pass. What is bright is what glows, so a sign glows, and so does the sign again where a wet road is mirroring it.
- **Indoors the hour stops driving the frame, and so does the sky.** A room is lit by its own fixtures at every hour of the day, is developed at its own exposure, and reflects `INDOORS.environment` (0.02) instead of whatever the dome was doing when the player walked in, with the sky's bearing held at zero because a room has none. Without that the room stood lit by the last sky the player saw outside, and the dome runs 85 to 1 between noon and midnight. Measured in the running game: 0.06 on the pavement at noon, 0.02 through the door, still 0.02 with the clock run on to midnight from inside, and back on the hour's own value on the way out. The copy of the sky is kept current while they are in there, so stepping back onto the pavement is not a frame of the sky they went in under.

- **This box adds no light to a room.** It used to add two, a hemisphere and a lamp from a fixed direction, and they were the same two in every room in the city whatever was over the player's head, which is the whole of what made a room read flat. A room is lit by the fixtures its own art drew, `@gb/scene` stands them up and gives the nearest twenty of them real point lights, and `indoors(true)` here only turns off a daylight a room cannot see. The room's own budget follows the player through it, off where their body is in the room's own metres, in the same frame call the city's does.

- **The indoor grade is developed for a lit interior, not inherited from daylight.** `INDOORS` used to be `DAY` with two fields moved, which left it with daylight's bloom threshold of 4. Nothing indoors reaches 4, so no lit thing in any room had ever glowed. The bloom's gate is a hard one, a `smoothstep` over a hundredth of a stop: over the threshold a pixel goes into the halo whole. So the number sits between the brightest thing in a room that is not a light and the dimmest thing that is, measured off `@gb/furnish`'s own inks and fixtures: a lit channel emits 2.57 in both languages, a screen 0.68 to 0.82, a printed line 1.11, and a pale worktop standing directly under a channel, the brightest lit surface a room makes, comes to 1.19. 1.7 is between the last two with room either side. The exposure did not move: the fixtures were set so a room lands on the level the flat fill developed it at, within a tenth of a stop on a frame weighted the way a first person view is. The shadows go half cold, because the fixtures are the warm bright end of the frame and the bounce off dark surfaces is all that is in them; full cold is the street's, where there is night air between the player and everything.
- The grade is a function of the hour and nothing else: no `Rng`, no wall clock, so the same minute of the same playthrough is the same frame on any machine.
- **The map is the grid the city was generated on**, handed to `@gb/hud` in cells: the plots as rectangles, each with its name for the hover and its charter's `prominence` for its fill, the player as an arrow at their own bearing, and a pin on every open step of every live quest, marked `main` or `side` so the story reads apart from the errands. Nothing is surveyed and nothing is baked, and it is measured only while the map is the face on screen, four times a second, so a window nobody has open costs nothing.
- **A step that names only a thing is still somewhere to go.** A place beats a person and a person beats a thing, so a delivery pins the stop rather than the parcel; a step to go and fetch something is pinned on the door of the building the thing is lying in, thing to room to building, because a shelf is inside a room and a room is measured in its own metres from its own corner. Any of an interchangeable pool answers, so three of the five crates is one pin.
- **A pin on somebody who is out goes where they will be found.** `@gb/crowd`'s `destination(npcId)` names the door a walker is heading for, so the pin and the route go to that building rather than to an empty room; somebody out with nowhere in particular to go is pinned where they stand, and everybody else at their post.
- **Indoors, the map and the route measure from the door the player came in by**, for the same reason: a room has its own metres from its own corner, and they are not the city's.
- **The quest being followed is one that is still running.** A job that has been handed in is not what the map pins or the guide walks to: the pins fall back to the first quest with an open step, the same way the objectives panel does, so the panel and the plan never disagree.
- **Only the places that earn it are written on the plan.** The places the player has walked into, the places the quests point at, **the places somebody is holding work in**, and the landmarks; every other building is a shape with a hover. Nine hundred labels on one map is not a map. A player who has taken nothing has no pins at all, so without the work the plan says nothing about where to start: measured on the `fifty` seed at 8 blocks, 16 quests in town, 0 pins, and 14 names on the plan of which none is a door that opens. The three doors with work in them are written on it. Asking the whole roster costs 0.178 ms a call on a 20 block city (20 people, 20 quests), four times a second while the map is up and nothing while it is not.
- **The way somewhere is the walk, not the line.** `G` asks `@gb/nav` for the route from where the player is standing, and answers with the distance along it and the compass point of its first stretch: "The Copper Wheel: 140 m, head north-east". The map is north up, so the two read together. Nowhere to walk to says so rather than pointing through a building, and a step that points at nobody and nowhere says that about the step rather than telling a player who is following a quest to go and find one.
- **A train is walked up to and picked off the plan.** `@gb/world` says which plots fast travel boards at and `@gb/kitbash` draws the entrance on that plot's doorstep, so the crosshair offers the station the player is standing at and the act key opens the plan on it. Every station in town is drawn on the plan and the one they are standing at is marked; picking another puts the veil up, and the ride lands on the frame after it, so the frame that dresses a neighbourhood nobody has been in is the one the veil covers and the veil comes off on the frame after that. The player and everybody walking with them land a step off the other doorstep, the same step the player opens their eyes on. A ticket to the station they are already standing at is not sold.
- **The compass is the body's yaw and the walk the guide found.** The strip is pushed which way the player faces, in radians clockwise from north, whenever it moves, and the tracked goal as that walk reads from where they are standing this frame: its bearing, the metres of route left, `main` or `side`. `@gb/nav` is asked for the whole walk, corner by corner, and never for its next corner alone: the corners behind the player are dropped as they go past them, so the arrow points at one still ahead and the distance counts down instead of climbing again the moment they turn a corner. The walk is asked for again when the quests change, when the player has strayed further than `STRAY` off it, and once a second otherwise, because a goal can move while they stand still. Between those, walking it is arithmetic over the corners (`src/route.ts`) and is done every frame. Indoors the strip goes, because a room has its own metres and the route is measured from the door.
- **The codex is `@gb/play`'s record in the world's words.** A place walked into is noted through `player.discover` on the way through the door; meeting somebody is `@gb/talk`'s to note; the town's story is told through `player.told` on the way in, every line of it as `@gb/forge` renders the premise, because what everybody in town knows is what the player arrives knowing. The tab is pushed the places by name with what the city was written to say about each (`Interior.description`, else what kind of place it is), the people with their role, their standing and every fact of their background (the learned ones in words and the rest as still to learn, a fact's id being its place in the background as `@gb/talk` earns them), and under History every line the player was told, its heading read off the line before the colon and a line with none drawn as heard.
- **The settings tab is the clock and the sky, and its intents are the keys' own calls.** `lock-time`, `skip-time` and `weather` go through the same `Conditions` `P`, `T` and `K` go through, and what the tab reads back is what the clock says.
- **Which AI runs which job is the service's, and both screens read it there.** The launcher's Settings and the settings tab in game draw the same providers and the same five jobs, with the same words, from one hand on `@gb/providers`. Either screen's six reports (`ai-model`, `ai-detail`, `ai-key`, `ai-health`, `ai-test`, `ai-job`) write to the service and both screens redraw from what it answers, so a provider set up at the front door is already set up in the tab. Nothing about a provider is kept in this browser, and a provider that is not configured says what it is waiting on rather than sitting blank.
- **A key goes one way, from either screen.** It is reported once, the field empties on the same tick, and it reaches the service and nothing else: it is never drawn back, never pushed to the interface, never written to storage and never read. Whether one is stored is the service's word, read off `secretSet` on the next answer.
- **A check and a test are slow, so the screen says it is asking.** Both mark the provider `checking` while the call is out, and neither can be pressed again while it is. A check that answers also lists what the provider can run, so the model becomes a list to pick from instead of a line to remember. A test is one real generation and its answer is drawn as it came: how long it took over what the model wrote, or why nothing came.
- **The quest log hears the clock before any job is taken, and on every frame after.** A timer counts from the last `clock` reading the log heard, and one that heard none fails on its first, so the first reading goes in from the constructor, before the first push and before a save is put back. Whatever a reading ends is announced, and the list is pushed with the failed page on it, `status: 'failed'` and its reason. The settings tab and the journal are pushed again once a game second while a page is counting down, so the time left moves, and once a game minute otherwise, so the hour reads right without a push a frame.
- **The quests tab is the quest log's own journal page, pushed as it stands.** Nothing here decides what a journal says: the page carries where every step stands, what it wants and how far along it is, and a step the player has not been told about is not on it, because the engine leaves it off. Walking the progress by hand instead is what listed a secret from the moment the quest was taken.
- **Giving a job up costs one report.** `@gb/hud` asks a second time on its own before it says so, so nothing here confirms it again; the quest log takes the job off and the list goes back without it, because the interface removes nothing itself.
- **A fork in a job is answered by its own key.** The question and the roads out of it are the quest's words, drawn by the interface; what comes back is the key of the road the player took, handed straight to the quest log, and the page and the objectives go back out with it.
- **The hour and the weather are the player's to turn.** `T` walks the time of day round dawn, midday, sundown and night, the stops sitting on the sun as `@gb/play` places it under the landscape's own sunrise and sunset (`SUNRISE_HOUR`, 12, `SUNSET_HOUR`, 21), so a jump to dawn lands with the sky lightening and the sun about to rise and a jump to sundown with it still up; `K` walks the weather round clear, overcast and rain; `P` holds the clock and lets it run again at the rate it was running at. Every one of them is a call into `@gb/play`, which owns what a reading means, and the sky follows because it reads the same clock every frame. Time only ever goes forward: a jump that wraps past midnight is tomorrow, so skipping to dawn runs a quest's timer down rather than winding it back.
- The interface is not in the chain. `@gb/hud` is DOM over the canvas, so the glow and the tint stop at the canvas edge and a panel is never bloomed.
- Half a game never sits on the page. A city that will not draw takes its stage and its interface back off before the panel says so, and the city itself can still be exported.
- The look belongs to `@gb/hud`. The panel is the one surface this box draws, because it has to be up before the hud exists; it is written from the hud's own tokens and holds no colour of its own.

## Reading a stall back

A frame over 120 ms says on the console where it went, worst part first, at most
one line every two seconds. The ten worst are also kept, and **F2** prints them
and empties the list, because a stall is over before anybody can reach the
console: walk the street that stuttered, then ask. It costs two clock reads a
part and says nothing on a frame that was fine, so it stays on in the running
game.

## What the chain costs

Measured in Chrome on this machine's WebGL2 fallback (no WebGPU), at 1920 by 1080 with a device pixel ratio of 1, standing on the pavement at 22:00 in a three block neon town. The scene pass was timed straight to the screen and then through the chain, alternately, over four runs.

| | render passes | draw calls | frame |
|---|---|---|---|
| scene straight to the screen | 2 | 92 | 2.7 to 3.4 ms |
| scene through the chain | 14 | 104 | 3.0 to 3.9 ms |

The chain is 0.25 to 0.6 ms, median 0.4. It buys twelve more passes: the luminance high pass, five mip levels blurred once across and once down, and the composite. All the blurring is at half resolution, none of it carries geometry, and the colour grade is a handful of instructions riding in the composite, so it costs nothing measurable of its own. Multisampling moved with it, off the frame buffer and onto the scene pass, and that move is inside the same number.

It is a pixel bill, so it is the same at noon and at midnight whatever is on screen, and it is four times as much at a device pixel ratio of 2.

## How to modify this blackbox safely

Anything with a rule in it belongs in another box. Keep `walk.ts` free of three.js so the movement maths stays testable. The panel's look is `docs/UI.md`, the one specification behind both surfaces of this game: the palette, the type, the chamfers and the durations are declared as custom properties on `.gb-boot` at the top of `index.html`, and nothing below that block writes a colour, a duration or a curve of its own. They are declared here rather than imported because `@gb/hud` declares its own on `.gb-hud` and arrives with the module bundle, long after the panel has to be on screen. Motion is `transform` and `opacity` only, it collapses to nothing under `prefers-reduced-motion`, and it never delays a click: the handler runs on the same tick and the entrance follows. A new way past a lock is a case in `src/locks.ts` and nowhere else, because the street door, the door inside the room and the gate of bars all ask it the same question. A new program on a screen is a branch in `src/programs.ts` over what the city already holds, never text written here. Nothing in `src/blueprint/` writes a colour, and only `massing.ts` and `pins.ts` may import three.js: what a plan comes out as (`plan.ts`, `cells.ts`) and how the camera moves (`orbit.ts`) are plain maths with tests over them, so the view can be judged in the browser and the rules cannot. The two surfaces that mount it are `boot/blueprint/blueprint.ts` for the launcher's preview and `src/citymap.ts` for the map in game; a change to the drawing has to be looked at in both. A new field on the form is a field in `index.html`, a name in `fields.ts` and a field on `CityBrief`; if the world file is to carry it, `@gb/world`'s `Asks` has to first, and if the form is to say it back, it is a key in `review.ts` and a `data-said` slot in the markup. The panel's two faces are two folds of the one card, `[data-boot="home"]` and `[data-boot="make"]`, and `Panel.face` is what shows one and takes the other out of the page: anything new on the front door belongs in one of those folds, so it is served with the first byte like the rest of it. A new thing the interface is told is a push in `reporting.ts`, or its own file when it has a clock of its own, as the compass and the chart do. Retuning how an hour looks, and how the inside of a building looks (its exposure, its glow, its tint and how much of the sky it reflects), is a change to the three records in `src/night.ts` alone; what actually lights a room is `@gb/furnish`'s fixtures and `@gb/scene`'s budget, and nothing here may add a light to one, and how much of the sky reaches a material is `reflectSky` there and nowhere else, so the renderer and the test bench cannot drift apart. How often the sky is copied again is `DRIFT` in `src/sky.ts` alone. A new step in the chain goes in `src/grade.ts`, and the cost in this contract is remeasured with it. Wiring belongs in the constructor and the constructor is testable: `tests/starting.test.ts` builds a real game against a stage with no GPU in it, walks the player up to somebody with the keys the game binds, and talks to them. Add to it rather than working around it. A new link in the art chain goes in `src/pack.ts`; anything a dressing answers only if it can carries itself through every wrapper here, because each one names what it answers and `src/seam.ts` carries the rest. Somebody's body is looked up through `src/members.ts` and nowhere else, so a new place bodies come from is another source handed to it rather than another lookup. Run `pnpm --filter @gb/app test`, `node tools/streaming-bench.ts 20` when anything on that chain or in the frame's `follow` moves, and `pnpm --filter @gb/app run dev` to look at it, which is the only way to judge a grade: stand on a pavement at 06, 12, 18 and 00 and read the frame, because a suite cannot tell you a street is flat.

## The panel: landing, wizard, shelf

Everything before there is a game is one card in `index.html` with two folds, `[data-boot="home"]` and `[data-boot="make"]`, and `Panel.face` shows one and takes the other out of the page.

`home` is a rail of four actions (create a game, create a city, import a city, settings) beside the shelf of cities already kept. A shelf card carries its name, its counts and whether a playthrough is in progress, and the last one played is marked.

`settings` is what belongs to the player rather than to any city, in three panels: the televisions (the address of a video, kept in this browser), the providers, and the five jobs. The last two are the settings tab in game, word for word, drawn in the panel's own shapes and reporting the same six intents to the same hand on `@gb/providers`. Until the service answers, the providers panel says it is reading; a service that never answers says so with the address it looked at, rather than standing empty.

`make` is the form in three steps, with the step strip in the foot: the city (theme, premise, the three style axes, size, how many instances open, how tall it may build), the writing (main quest, side jobs, tone), and reading it back before it is built. Every step reads and writes the same `CityBrief`; the step strip only decides which fields are on screen. Each step plate carries a chevron mark in the plate's own colour, whole on the step being read and quiet on the others.

Step one's summary panel holds four readouts (blocks, zones, instances, tallest building) and five actions in one grid: Generate with AI, Save draft, Generate the city, Preview blueprint, and Next: the writing across the foot of it.

A `[data-write="<field>"]` button has the model write that field; `all` asks for the five together. A `[data-said="<key>"]` slot is filled from the brief by `review.ts`, so a new readout is a key there and a slot here, never a number worked out in the markup. A `[data-note]` line inside a `[data-notes]` group is where the controls in that group say what they could not do; `notes.ts` writes it.

`Save draft` goes through `kept.ts`, beside the settings and the saves. `Generate the city` and `Preview blueprint` go through `PanelHandlers.plan` and `PanelHandlers.preview`, both optional: `Boot` implements `plan` over `CityMaker.plan` and keeps the `World` on `Boot.laid`, and `preview` over `Boot.preview()`. A handler that is not there is a plain line saying so, never a reported success.

A whole step drawn in front of the card goes on `[data-boot="stage"]`, the other child of the `.gb-boot` section. `Panel.stage(on)` puts it up and hands back the element: the card leaves the page and the accessible tree with it, Escape belongs to the step rather than to the panel, and taking it down empties the stage, brings the card back exactly as it was and puts the keyboard on the tile that opened it. The blueprint is the one thing drawn there, so it sits under `.gb-boot` and is painted from the same custom properties as the rest of the front door: `boot/blueprint/palette.ts` reads them off the mounted element and hands the renderer numbers. A page with no palette on it is a sentence on the tile, not a black screen.

Any control or row with `data-hint="..."` streams that sentence into the reserved line under the rail on hover and focus. The line holds its height whether or not it has words in it, so nothing below it moves.

Icons are `[data-icon="<name>"]`, drawn from `src/boot/icons.ts`. A new action is a `button.gb-hud-btn` with `data-boot`, an icon and a hint. A new field is a row in the matching section plus a name in `fields.ts` and a field on `CityBrief`; if the world file is to carry it, `@gb/world`'s `Asks` has to first.

The palette, the type, the chamfers and the durations are custom properties on `.gb-boot` at the top of `index.html`, and nothing below that block writes a colour, a duration or a curve of its own.
