# @gb/forge contract

contractVersion: 0.27.0

## Purpose

Builds a whole city from one brief: its history first, with the kinds of place that history invents declared beside the presets, then the streets, the plots the history calls for and a station every five hundred metres among them, the handful of places that open (three, whatever the city's size) with the locks their charters put on them and the screens on their desks, the people standing in them with their own lives and the keys in their pockets, the things lying around and what they cost, a home for sale with its deed on a counter, and the quests that string them together, then checks all of it before handing it over. Then grows a finished one: doors that were painted on open, new land goes up at the edge, and both get work written over them.

## Inputs

| Param | Schema | Preconditions |
|---|---|---|
| `Forge.build(brief)` | [schema/brief.json](schema/brief.json) | theme (60 characters) and seed are yours; blocks across and down (20 by default, a city rather than a hamlet), the places that open (`openPlaces`, 3 by default, 24 at most), density and max storeys have defaults; cells per block and roads out (1 to 4) are picked from the seed if you leave them out. Blocks go as high as the grid holds, below. `brief` (the owner's own words, unbounded) and `asks` (`@gb/world`'s `Asks`) are optional: both reach the history writer and go into the file, and `asks` reaches the quest writer |
| `new Forge(narrator)` | a `Narrator` | answers `nameCity`, `writeQuests`, and `writeInstances` for every place that opens at once, each request carrying `has`, the instance brief (below). A narrator with no plural answers the three single-place questions (`namePlace`, `describeNpc`, `describeItem`) instead and gets the same city, with every question about every place in the air at once. Every request about a place carries its `kind` (the charter's word), the `charter` itself, and the `street` its door is on, so a narrator can write a jail knowing what a jail is here and where it stands; `namePlace` and `describeNpc` are also handed the town's story as `premiseLines` renders it. `describeNpc` may answer `life` and `background` in `@gb/world`'s own shapes; a narrator that leaves them out gets a person who answers off `personality` and `knowledge` alone. `writePremise` is optional: a narrator that answers it gets a city built around the `History` it wrote (the premise, plus any `charters` it declares), and one that does not gets a town with no story, built from the presets. `namePlaces` is optional: a narrator that answers it hangs the signs over the doors that do not open, all asked for in one call, and one that does not gets them written here. `writeQuests` carries `from`, how many quests the city already hands out, so a growth's work carries on from the last id instead of colliding with it; absent is a city being written for the first time |
| `Forge.extend(world, growth)` | a `@gb/world` `World`; `growth` is a count of new buildings, or `{ blocks, places }` | `blocks` needs empty land touching a sidewalk; `places` needs frontage standing with nothing behind its door. Both are drawn against the history the city carries, so a town grows into the town it already was |
| `Forge.extendQuests(world, quests)` | a grown `World`; the quests it already hands out | the world is the one `extend` grew. Ids carry on past the ones in `quests` |

## Outputs

| Param | Schema | Postconditions |
|---|---|---|
| `build` | `{ world, quests, rejected, dropped }` | `world.check()` is empty; every quest passed `@gb/quest` validation; `rejected` lists the ones that did not, with why; `dropped` lists the kinds of place the history declared that the city would not take, with why. The world carries its charters (`world.charters()`: the fourteen presets and whatever the history invented, every one resolved), the street grid, the plots and the road graph, roads out included. Exactly `openPlaces` plots carry an interior, whatever the size of the city, and everything else is frontage; a town too small to hold that many and still be mostly frontage gets fewer. Every plot is inside `@gb/world`'s `PLOT_BAND`. Every person carries a `life` and a staged `background`; every item carries a `value`, and the ones on a counter are for sale (below). Every locked door names its key, which is in the pocket of somebody in front of it, or carries a code; every screen carries a `machine`; a home is for sale with its deed on a counter; a station boards every five hundred metres (below) |
| `extend` | plot ids added | the ids of the buildings put up on empty land, and nothing else: a facade that opened is a plot the city already had, and which ones opened is read off the world (`world.interiors()` the base had not). Every base record is byte for byte as it was, the city's history included, but for the one field a facade gains when its door opens (`plot.interiorId`) |
| `extendQuests` | `{ quests, rejected }` | every quest passed `@gb/quest` validation against the whole grown city, so a pack's errand may name the base's people and places; `rejected` lists the drafts that did not, with why. Ids continue past the ones handed in |
| `summarise(world, premise?)` | `WorldSummary` | the abstract world a quest writer reads: what the town is about, what the owner asked of the writing (`asks`, off `world.asks()`), its places by the word of their kind, who is in them and which room, what is there and what it costs, where each street door stands in metres, a surface inside each place something can be left on, its `locks` (each with the room behind it, its key and who carries it, its code, and what lies behind it), its `machines` (program, lock, code), what it is `forSale` for, and the `work` done in it |
| `BLOCKS_MAX` | a number | the most blocks a side a brief may ask for, 116 today. It is `@gb/world`'s `MAX_GRID_SIDE` read in blocks, so anything putting a limit in front of a person reads it here rather than writing a number of its own |
| `questTargets(objectives)` | a set of npc ids | who a quest is waiting on right now: every person named on an open line of `QuestLog.objectives()`. The running game keeps them at their posts whatever share of the town it sends out walking, and the harness measures it against that rule |

## Errors (closed set)

- `invalid-brief`: the brief failed its schema, or the city it asks for is one no world will hold (the widest grid those blocks could need is over `@gb/world`'s `MAX_GRID_SIDE`, 2,048 cells a side). Nothing is built, and no world constructor throws. The size bound is not expressible in JSON Schema, so it lives in the brief's own check rather than in `schema/brief.json`.
- `unsound-world`: the generator produced a world that fails its own integrity check. Carries the problems; this is a bug in the generator, never a bad brief.

A narrator writing an unusable quest is not an error: those quests are dropped and reported in `rejected`. A narrator writing an unusable history is not an error either: each charter it declares is checked on its own and dropped with its reason (reported in `dropped`), and the premise is salvaged field by field against `@gb/world`'s `premiseContract` (a kind no charter declares is dropped from `build`, a side without both its parts is dropped, a line that is not a line is dropped); one nothing can be salvaged from is dropped whole and the town is built without one.

## Dependencies

- `@gb/kit` contract (game/kit/CONTRACT.md): deterministic rng, ids, results.
- `@gb/world` contract (game/world/CONTRACT.md): everything it builds into.
- `@gb/quest` contract (game/quest/CONTRACT.md): validation of what the narrator writes.
- `@gb/kitbash` contract (game/kitbash/CONTRACT.md): `RECIPES`, the wall pieces a frontage and an openness are built from, written into every charter the history declares.

## Invariants

- Geometry is arithmetic, never invention: streets, sidewalks, plot footprints, entrances, rooms, furniture and anchors are computed. A narrator supplies names, personalities, quest logic, and the axes of any kind of place it invents.
- **No kind of place is a word this box knows.** Everything a kind decides (its share of the mix, its storeys, its rooms, who stands in it, what it keeps, what its signs say, what its people say about it) is read off its charter, and the charters are `world.charters()`: `@gb/world`'s fourteen presets plus whatever the history declared, every one resolved (`src/charters/`) and written into the file before the first plot. A theme leans on traits (`src/theme/traits.ts`: somewhere that pours, somewhere people live stacked up, a civic hall with no counter), never on words, so a jail a history invents is weighed the way its axes say. Every per-kind draw is forked on the word, so declaring one more kind of place moves nothing another kind already rolled. Measured: a history declaring `jail` gets a town with jails of 2 storeys and up, one of them open, with a duty desk, cells with beds, a guard on the door and a receptionist behind the desk, a sign, and people repeating the charter's own rumours; the offline neon history "the clubs" declares a `disco` and a 3x3 town on it has 3, one of them open (`The Ladder Club`: a doorman, a second guard, a bartender, four drinkers, two dancing).
- **A charter goes through a gate before a plot takes its word.** One that fails `@gb/world`'s `CharterSchema` is dropped with the fields that failed it; one whose rooms plan into an interior nobody can stand in is dropped with that reason; one past the twenty-fourth is dropped; the rest stand, the premise's `build` is stripped of the dropped words, and every reason comes back on `dropped`. Dropping without reporting is the failure this exists to remove.
- **A whole town is planned before a narrator is asked anything.** Every site's kind, height and openness, every open building's rooms, furniture and anchors, who is standing where and what is lying about are all decided with nothing awaited. Then one call asks about every place that opens at once, and the answers are written into the world in planned order. A city of 2,489 buildings costs 24 descriptive calls, the same order as a town of 60, because what opens is a number the city carries rather than a share of its plots, and all of them can be in the air at once instead of one at a time.
- **Reassembled by index, never by arrival.** `writeInstances` hands back one answer per request in request order, so the nth open building takes the nth answer; inside an answer a person is matched to a post by `postId` and a name to a thing by `thingId`, never by position. Ids are minted in the plan (rooms, doors, props, anchors, machines, and the keys the doors name) and in the assembly loop (plots, people, the rest of the things) and nowhere else, both of which run in planned order, so everything is numbered the same whatever the narrator's concurrency was. A person written for a post that is not there is dropped, and a post nobody wrote is left empty.
- **A narrator with no plural is asked everything at once.** `src/narrator/one-at-a-time.ts` issues `namePlace` for every open place with `Promise.all`, then every `describeNpc` and `describeItem` of a place the same way, and writes each answer into the slot of the request it answered. The offline narrator draws every answer off the request's own index (a name off the roster, a sign off the pool, a life off `npc/<index>`), so nothing it writes depends on which question came first. Measured with a narrator that answers slowly: 32 questions in the air at once over a 3x3 town of 16 people and 3 open places, and the same city to the byte as one answered in order.
- **Every street has a name, and every door knows its street.** `src/narrator/streets.ts` deals one name per band off the seed and the theme's own words (a noun, an adjective or a family name, then Street, Row or Lane; an avenue is called one), columns west to east then rows north to south, so no two streets in a town share a name and a town with more streets than words numbers the rest. The bands are read back off the road graph the world carries (`src/layout/lines.ts`), so a building `extend` adds later stands on the street it always did. Every `PlaceRequest` and `InstanceRequest` carries the `street` its door is on, and a numbered sign is that door's address. Measured on three 3x3 offline towns: 178 of 178 requests carried a street, 35 of 35 numbered signs named the street their door stands on.
- **A sign over a door nobody opens is written here.** All but three buildings are frontage, and naming each of them was more than half of a build's descriptive calls spent on a string the player reads off the street and nothing else. `src/narrator/signs.ts` writes those; the narrator names only the places it writes whole.
- **No word heads two signs in a town.** `src/narrator/name-pool.ts` deals every head word a town can put on a sign out once: the theme's first names, family names, adjectives, nouns and place words, the words the town's own story is told in (`src/narrator/vocabulary.ts` takes the nouns out of the premise and the theme), and forty numbered addresses, shuffled once off the seed. The nth plot takes the nth head, so a plot's sign depends on nothing but its number: the shut signs written here and the open places the offline narrator names come off the same pool by the same number and cannot collide, whatever order the answers land in. A town bigger than the pool numbers the rest, and a number heads no other sign. Measured over ten 4x4 towns on ten themes, 1,225 plots: 0 repeated head words.
- **More than one shape of sign.** The head's own list decides the shape: a first name with the trade (Mara's Kitchen, Rook's), a family name with its heirs or its trade (Endicott & Daughters, Fane Repairs, Lomax's), a place word with the trade plainly stated (Harbour Supply, Wharf Cafe, Kiln Street Rooms), a numbered address on the street the door is on (12 Kettle Row), and The X Y off an adjective or a noun. The trade is the kind's own word, so a sign says what the place is. Five shapes measured over those ten towns: 387 possessives, 248 plain trades, 233 numbers, 214 The X Y, 143 family firms.
- An interior is planned in that order: rooms, then doors, then furniture, then the places people stand. The entry room touches the wall the street door is in; every other room hangs off it rather than off another room, so nobody walks through a bedroom to reach the kitchen.
- One door from the street, one door between rooms, and every room reachable from the street door.
- Furniture never lands in a doorway, never overlaps another piece, and never seals off a door or a place somebody stands. Every piece is tested against the free floor before it lands, so a building that is too small simply holds less.
- **Every prop is the size `@gb/world` says it is.** `PROP_SPECS` there is the one table of furniture sizes, cells, contact heights and which pieces stand on a counter top; `src/interior/props.ts` reads its footprints in metres off `footprintOf` and holds no size of its own, so a chair the planner keeps a table's width off a table is a chair drawn that width off it. What this box adds is `SEAT_SPECS`, the back rest and pad of every seat, measured off the drawn triangles.
- **Every plot is inside `@gb/world`'s `PLOT_BAND`**: 3 to 6 cells of frontage, 5 to 8 of depth, 1 to 4 storeys, read in the door's frame. `src/layout/plots.ts` cuts to the band's own numbers, the strip depth is clamped to it, a brief's `maxStoreys` is clamped to it, and `extend` drops in sites that are inside it whichever wall the door lands on. Measured at `--cells 12`, the size that cut 37 plots outside the band before: 0 of 73 outside, and 0 at 6, 40 and the seed's own choice.
- **A doorway keeps 1.2 m across the opening and a metre either side of the wall, and the street door keeps 1.6 m inside it.** Somebody walking in off the street is put down a stride into the room and has to land on floor, not in a chair, so the band inside that one door is deeper than the band an inside door keeps for people passing through.
- **Everything in a room except a rug is something a body walks round**, a chair and a bar stool included: a seat is half a metre of furniture the player collides with. A row of tables with chairs drawn up to them leaves 0.45 m gaps, which is not a gap a 0.7 m body fits through, so the walk test has to see the chairs or it will call a room connected that is sealed in half. A way through is measured with every piece grown by the player's radius and half a grid cell, so an aisle has to be wider than the grid's own rounding rather than exactly as wide as it.
- Every anchor is somewhere a person can get to, and close enough to do the job. Anyone sitting or sleeping is on their own seat or bed; nobody stands in a doorway, inside somebody else's furniture, or on floor cut off from the doors. Anyone working faces what they work at: staff behind their counter, a cook at the stove, a browser at the case, a seat drawn up to its table.
- How close a body stands is the reach of what it is doing, not the width of a walking body. Hands on a surface (serving, cooking, working a bench) means 0.15 m of floor between the body and the face of the piece, because the standing clips put the hands 0.02 to 0.13 m in front of the body at about 1.03 m up: further back and the forearms rest on air. Facing a piece without touching it (a browser at a case, somebody at a sink or an altar) means 0.3 m. The bands live in `src/interior/stance.ts` and nowhere else, and every anchor is measured against them in the tests, in metres, off the piece's own footprint.
- Standing that close is standing inside the skirt the walk grid keeps clear round anything solid. A body may be inside the skirt of the one piece it is working at, never another's, and still has to have walkable floor within a step of its back, so the place it stands is a place it could have walked to.
- A body on a seat is not at the seat's centre. The sitting clip holds the body well behind its own root: the pelvis 0.33 m back and the back of the widest coat in the wardrobe 0.50 m back, measured off `Sitting_Idle_Loop` in `assets/dist/anims.glb` skinned onto all twelve dressed characters. So a root at the centre of a chair puts the back rest through the torso. The anchor goes forward of the seat instead, along the way the seat faces, far enough that the body's back is against the front face of the back rest with 2 cm to spare; a seat with nothing to lean on centres the pelvis on the pad. That is a different number for every seat, because the backs are at different depths:

  | seat | back rest face | pad | anchor, forward of the centre |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | chair | 0.194 | -0.220 to 0.220 | 0.326 |
  | office-chair | 0.235 | -0.232 to 0.232 | 0.285 |
  | sofa | 0.370 | -0.402 to 0.242 | 0.150 |
  | bar-stool | none | -0.200 to 0.200 | 0.330 |
  | bed | 0.950 | -0.970 to 0.867 | lying: 0.052, the middle of the pad |

  Metres from the piece's own centre, positive towards its back. Every piece a body sits or lies on is in that table and nothing else is. The seat numbers are measured off the triangles `@gb/furnish` draws in both interior languages and live in `src/interior/props.ts`; the body numbers and the rule live in `src/interior/stance.ts`. The pelvis has to land on the pad whatever the rule says, and every seat is held to that in the tests.
- A body **lying down** is centred on its own root: the lying clip reaches 0.96 m either way from it, so nothing about a headboard or a back rest applies and the root goes on the middle of the pad, the headboard behind the head rather than against the back. `@gb/furnish` measures its mattress at 1.970 m of level pad in the corporate language and 1.934 m in the domestic one, so a 1.92 m body lies inside it; centring is what keeps whatever margin there is even at both ends.
- **A body seated at a desk reaches for it too.** The seated desk pose puts the wrists 0.20 to 0.24 m in front of the root at 0.78 m off the floor, against a 0.75 m desk top, so the near edge of the desk belongs inside that band and outside the body. `AT_DESK` in `src/interior/stance.ts` is that band (0.05 to 0.20 m, planned at 0.115), the stance `work-desk` is held to the way `serve` is held to `AT_HAND`, and the chair is drawn up until the root stands there: hands on the top and knees under it. `@gb/cast` measures the pose; every desk in every plan is held to the band in the tests.
- **A drinker sits on a bar stool.** `@gb/cast`'s stool clips put the hips on a 0.75 pad with the feet on the rail and the same root-to-hips offset the chair clip has, so a stool is a seat like any other, placed like a chair 0.30 m up: the stool's root lands 2.5 cm off the counter's face and the stool sits its own offset behind that. `sit-drink` is that stance and belongs on a stool and nothing else (on a chair it floats 0.30 m), so the tables in a bar seat `sit` bodies, and every stool in every plan is held to that in the tests.
- Everybody is one of the two bodies the pack ships, `male` or `female`, drawn from the interior's own stream.
- **Dancers only where the town's story calls for dancing.** `callsForDancing` in `src/premise/wants.ts` reads the theme and the premise for dancing, clubs and nightclubs; a taproom in such a town gets up to two `dance` anchors on the open floor before its tables go in, each keeping 0.9 m of floor, and a town whose story says nothing of it has nobody dancing. The offline neon trade "the clubs" asks for it and declares the `disco` (`src/premise/places.ts`): a taproom behind a door with a doorman, whose people dance because the town's story says so, admitting people only so far, its cellar shut. Measured on the offline 3x3 neon town `club-1`: 3 discos, one open (`The Ladder Club`): a doorman and a second guard, a bartender, four drinkers, 2 dance anchors with two dancing, the cellar locked behind a gate of bars with its key in the doorman's pocket, a camera over the door, and a snake screen on the bar. The dance floor is a `taproom` dressed for dancing; a room use of its own is handed to `@gb/world`.
- **A place that keeps watch has somebody on the door.** A charter whose `work` includes `watch` gets a `guard` post a stride inside its street door when its rooms placed none, and that post is filled like a counter, so a jail's warder and a disco's doorman are there whatever the dice say.
- A body **propped against a wall** is not at the wall either. The lean clips hold it 0.414 m behind its own root, measured off all three `Idle_Wall*` clips skinned onto all twelve dressed characters, so a root on the wall is a body through it. The anchor goes 0.44 m out from the inside face instead, facing the room, which leaves 2.6 cm. Nothing stands under a propped body, so it claims its own floor rather than a piece's: 0.79 m out from the wall by 0.76 m along it, out of the doorway band, clear of anything already against that wall, and kept for it so a chair drawn up afterwards does not land on its feet. The number lives in `src/interior/stance.ts` beside the seat offsets, and `@gb/cast` measures the same one from the clips.
- Whether a seat is a place somebody can sit is a question about the seat, so nothing about which anchors are accepted turns on that offset: reachability, doorways and other people's furniture are all judged at the seat, exactly as they were when the body sat on its centre. Moving a body onto its seat properly cannot cost a town a single anchor.
- A city is mostly frontage. Every plot is a building with a door, a sign and a name; a fixed handful of them also have an interior, and the rest cannot be walked into. A plot without an interior is closed all the way through: nobody is stationed in it, nothing is lying about in it, and no quest step points into it, so a closed door offers the player nothing rather than teasing them with something they cannot reach.
- **A city's size is scenery: the places it opens are an absolute number.** `openPlaces` is the brief's, three by default, and it is the same three in a hamlet and in a city of seven thousand buildings. Everything a player meets follows from it, so a big city costs about what a small one costs to build, to send to somebody and to learn. Measured on one seed at 2, 10, 20, 32 and 50 blocks: 38, 785, 3,199, 7,486 and 20,523 buildings, 3 places every time, 13 to 14 people, 9 quests, 0.08 s to 1.56 s, 0.07 MB to 4.84 MB. The number is the town's, never the batch's, so building a city bigger opens no more doors. A town too small to hold three and still be mostly frontage opens fewer, and a town of one or two buildings opens one anyway, because a town with no door at all is not a town. Growing a finished city is the other case, below: a growth is an authored addition and it says how many more doors the city opens.
- Which doors open is never a list of kinds. Each charter is weighed by what its own rooms turn out to make (`src/interior/draw.ts`, memoised on the word and the charter's digest, so two cities in one process that invent one word differently share nothing): a counter that is always staffed counts most, then anybody else who works there, then loose stock, then whether the place reads as somewhere people are. A kind a history invents is weighed the same way. On top of that a plot with room behind its door scores higher, because three places have to hold a whole city's cast, its stock and its work; a plot near the middle of town scores higher, and a plot on an avenue higher again, because those are the doors a player tries; and a seeded nudge wide enough to let a chapel on the square in ahead of a shop at the ring road, so a town is not a list of its businesses. Measured on one 8x8 frontier seed: 555 plots, 3 doors, a workshop, a bar and a home.
- **The doors that open stand a walk apart.** Every pick has to be further from the doors already open than the town's longest side divided by one more than the number of places, so three places are three parts of the city rather than three shops on one corner. The spacing gives way where the town has nothing else to offer, because a door on the wrong corner beats no door at all. Measured on a 20-block city: three places 308, 360 and 572 m apart in 1,210 m of town.
- **A city's doors are as many different places as it can manage.** Every door of a kind already open charges the next one of that kind, up to one tier of what a place has to offer, so a brief that asks for more places spends them across the town rather than down one column of the ranking. The charge is capped for a reason: a repeat gives way to the shop or the surgery that has not opened yet, never to a lock-up. Six restaurants is one restaurant met six times. Measured on one 6x6 seed: a brief asking for 9 places gets 8 kinds, one asking for 24 gets 12.
- **A city takes the things a town needs, in the order it needs them, then the story's own place, then a home.** Somewhere to sit down, somewhere to buy something, somewhere to sleep, somewhere to work, somewhere to live: each is a question about what a place holds, not about what it is called, so a nightclub answers the first and a hospital the third without anything being added here. A place bought for one of them answers the next one too, so a restaurant is somewhere to sit down, somewhere to buy something and somewhere to work all at once and three doors are not spent on one answer. Then a kind of place the town's own history demands takes a door of its own, because that is the door the story means the player to try, on the best plot of that kind and no other (scoring every plot of a demanded kind fills a town with hotels). Then a home, because a home is what the player buys. Three doors is one counter, the story's place and a home; a brief asking for more works down the list and spreads the rest.
- A piece that belongs on a counter top stands on one. `PROP_SPECS` marks those pieces, and the planner puts each one over the back of a counter already in the room, facing the way that counter faces, with `Furniture.lift` set to that counter's own top: `METRICS.furniture.serviceCounterHeight` for a service counter, `barCounterHeight` for a bar. It claims no floor, because the floor under it is the counter's. So a bar and a shop have a till on the counter and a cafe has its machine, and nothing is left standing beside the counter it belongs on.
- The interior varies with the seed and stays identical for the same one: an entrance hall or none, service rooms across the back or down one side, counters on either hand, furniture swept along the walls until it fits.
- Each kind of building is recognisable from the inside: a bar has a counter you can walk behind with stools along it, a shop a counter you queue at and cases to browse, a house a sofa facing a screen and a bed against a wall.
- **A room the public stands in usually has a screen on the wall.** A taproom, a cafe, a dining room, a shop floor, a hotel lobby, a waiting room and a concourse each get one about three times in five; a living room always has one facing the sofa; a kitchen, a store room and a bedroom never do. Not every room, because a set in every one reads as a rule rather than as a place. It goes up after the propped bodies have taken their walls and before the floor fills, and it claims a quarter of a metre of floor. Measured over six towns: 11 sets across 18 open places.
- A room people drink in is not a room of chairs. A bar and a cafe put one or two bodies propped against their free walls before the tables go in, on two different walls rather than side by side, and a propped body is a post like any other. Where everybody sits down and nobody stands, a room reads as a waiting room.
- Same seed, same city, down to the byte, whatever order a narrator's answers land in and however many of them are in the air at once. Sub-streams are forked by label off a root the generator never draws from: `streets` for the town plan, `avenues` off that for where the spines run, `plots` for the mix of buildings, `quests` for how much work the town has in it, then one per block, per site and per interior. A new stream is a new label, never a draw from an existing one, so adding one cannot shift what another already decided.
- Nothing a narrator writes is trusted: quests are validated against the world and dropped if they do not hold up.
- **Every post in an open place is filled.** A city opens three doors and its whole cast stands behind them, so an empty stool is a third of the people the player will ever meet missing: every anchor a charter gives a role to gets somebody. Who stands at each is read off the charter (`roleFor` in `src/populate.ts`: stalls get a vendor, a desk a receptionist, a counter with a bench behind it a mechanic, one that pours or feeds a bartender, any other a clerk), still one of `@gb/world`'s closed roles. What lies about is the archetypes of the classes the charter holds, through `@gb/world`'s `HOLDING_ARCHETYPES`.
- Nobody in a town shares a name, a personality or a script: names, habits and what a person knows come from a vocabulary the theme picks, not from one template. The offline narrator's names come off a roster (`src/narrator/roster.ts`): every first name against every family name, shuffled once off the seed, and the nth person takes the nth pair, so nobody in a city shares a name and the answer depends on nothing but the person's number.
- **Every person has a life, and a codex to earn.** `src/narrator/lives.ts` writes `npc.life` in `@gb/world`'s shape: `reason` (why they are at this spot, first person, one sentence talk can say out loud: "I keep the bar at Hedge & Sons; somebody has to be behind it while the doors are open"), `errand` (what they are doing when they walk, first person, naming no place because `@gb/crowd` publishes where), `history` (where they came from, what they did before, and what the town's own turn did to them, in the premise's words), `interests`, `manner`, `cares` and `avoids`, the last two sometimes the town's own argument. `src/narrator/background.ts` writes `npc.background`, five or six staged facts: the job (`met`), the history a sentence at a time and the interest (`talked`), what they care about (`quest`), and one thing only ever heard from somebody else (`told`). Written in the schema's own key order so a file read back is the same bytes. `@gb/scribe` fills the same fields from the model.
- **A counter sells what it owns.** Every item lying in a place with a `serve` anchor belongs to the person on it (`ownerNpcId`) and carries `value`, the price that counter sells it for, from `src/prices.ts`: a going rate per archetype (a cup 3, a crate 40, a gem 300) moved up to 30% either way by the seed. So who sells what is in the world document already: the seller is the owner, the price is the value, and taking it without paying is what a theft is. An item in a house has a value and no seller.
- Buildings leave gaps. `extend` fills them, and never moves anything already placed. What it does to a city that is already finished is below.
- **A road is as wide as its class**, from `@gb/world`'s `METRICS.road`, so the width a car drives and the width the city is laid with are one number per class. A street band is 2 cells of pavement, 5 of roadway and 2 of pavement (18 m); an avenue band is 2, 7 and 2 (22 m); the road out of the valley is 9 cells of roadway (18 m) with 2 of pavement each side. Cells are 2 m and the mountain ring is 4; rooms and furniture are sized in metres from the same `METRICS`.
- Every roadway is an odd number of cells, so a band's centreline is a line of cell centres. That is where the road graph's nodes go, and it is what lets a wider road be laid without moving a junction off the middle of the road.

### The city's history

Nothing is placed until the town has one. `writePremise({theme, seed})` is the first call the generator makes, before a single plot, and everything after it is written against the answer.

**The shape is `@gb/world`'s `Premise`, not this box's.** A history is a fact about the city, so the city carries it: it goes into `World.found` and comes back out of `World.premise()`, and whoever opens a file somebody sent them can read what the town is about with no generator present. This box writes one and builds against it.

- **Why the town is here** (`livesOn`), **what happened to it** (`happened`), **what is at stake** (`stake`), **who is arguing about it** (`sides`, two at least, each with a name and what they want), **what everybody knows** (`common`), and **what the town therefore holds** (`build`, as `moreOf`, `fewerOf` and `mustHave`, each a word of one of the city's charters).
- It is short on purpose. Every later prompt carries the whole of it, and a premise long enough to bloat a context is a premise nobody reads.
- It is in kinds of place on purpose. A history that names no buildings changes no city, and a stage that costs a model call and changes no city is worse than no stage.

What reads it: the town is often named after what it lives on; the mix is pushed towards what the history wants and away from what it does not; a demanded kind goes up on a seeded site and its door is ranked as worth opening as a counter with somebody behind it; every place that opens is written knowing the town; and the main line is about what is at stake, between the two sides, bound to the two counters it forks over.

- **It declares the kinds of place it needs.** A history may carry `charters` beside the premise (`src/premise/shape.ts`, `History`): each one `@gb/world`'s `CharterSchema`, a composition over the closed axes and nothing invented but the word. `build` may then name those words as well as the presets'. Offline, a trade or a turn in `premise/*.md` names what it `declares` out of `src/premise/places.ts`.
- **Determinism.** The history is drawn from its own labelled stream, so a town with one is the same town every time and a town without one is the town it always was. Two histories on one seed are two towns: on one 5x5 coastal seed, 99 of 181 buildings come out a different kind and 8 of 19 doors open somewhere else.
- **It goes into the file, and `extend` reads it back.** A city grown later fills its gaps with the same kind of town it started as. The same city on the same land with the same hand of dice grows seven wharf-side buildings with its history and one without it.
- **Nothing a narrator writes is trusted, and nothing good in it is thrown away for one bad word.** `src/premise/history.ts` reads a history in the order its parts depend on each other: the charters first, each checked on its own and put through the gate, then the premise against `@gb/world`'s `premiseContract`, salvaged field by field in `src/premise/check.ts`: a kind no charter declares is dropped from `build`, a side without both its name and its wants is dropped, a line that is not a line is dropped, and the rest is checked again. One that still fails (one side, a stake longer than the document holds, prose) is dropped whole and the town is built as if nobody had written one. Checking it here is what keeps a long one out of `World.found`, where it would take the whole city with it instead of just itself.
- **Offline it is composed from the seed.** `premise/trades.md` is what a town can live on, `premise/turns.md` is what can have happened to it; both halves push the same mix, both carry their own words, and either may declare a kind of place. There is one for every kind of town.

### What a town is made of

The theme is read as one of seven kinds of town (frontier, coastal, industrial, neon, alpine, agrarian, or plain when it names none of them), and that kind decides the place, not just the words on the sign. Its own history then pushes that further. Every kind of place in it is one of the city's charters.

- **The mix.** Every charter has its `share`, the theme multiplies it through the charter's traits (a neon city stacks flats and desks, a mining town leans on places that pour and civic halls with no counter), the town's history multiplies it again (a collapsed trade keeps its sheds and loses its offices), and the seed swings each kind up or down and drops up to two kinds the town turns out not to have at all, every draw forked on the kind's own word. Nothing the history asks for is ever dropped. A residential charter is never dropped, never swung below what the theme asks for and never pushed down by the history, because whatever happened here, people still live here. No kind is ever squeezed below a weight of one, so on a kind the theme already keeps rare the history's "fewer of" has nothing left to take.
- **The keystone and the staples.** Every town has its keystone, wherever the dice fall: one place everybody passes through, which is also where the main line starts. It is the staffed charter with somewhere to sit, one that pours before one that does not, the lowest word of those: the bar, until a history declares something ahead of it. Whatever the history demands goes up beside it, which is how "a surgery, because of the flood" becomes a building rather than a sentence. One to three more places the town can be known for (a charter with a service, or one the street notices) go up alongside those, each keyed off its own word and the theme's lean on it, and all of them stand on seeded sites rather than the first sites in the list.
- **The storeys.** A charter's `size.storeys` is the range, clamped to the brief's and the band's; a building on an avenue stands a storey taller.
- **The rooms.** A charter's `rooms` are the programme: a hall inside the door when the building is deep enough (a foyer for an `entrance-hall`, a lobby's depth for anything else), the main room behind it, the service rooms across the back or down one side. Every room is cut under `@gb/world`'s label for its use and dressed by the one routine that use names (`src/interior/furnish/index.ts`, one case per value of `ROOM_USES`): a `ward` puts as many beds along its walls as its floor holds, an `assembly` ranks seats before a piece at the front with somebody standing at it facing them.
- **The people.** Names, habits and what somebody knows are drawn from the theme's vocabulary: a shared core plus the words that flavour uses, so two towns under one theme are not the same cast twice. What they say of the place they stand in is the charter's own rumours, falling through to what everybody in town knows.
- **The signs.** A charter's `names` say what its signs are called: the trade words its templates state plainly (`{family} Coffee` gives Coffee), whether a sign may be nothing but a name (`{family}'s`), and the templates that open on an adjective or a family name, filled as written. The head word still comes off the town's one pool by plot number, so nothing about the charter can put one word over two doors.

### The instance brief

A place's plan says what it contains beyond its people and its stock, so a quest can be built on it: a room behind a lock and what opens it, a screen and what it runs, a camera, a sale. Everything below is decided in `src/interior/` from the charter's axes and nothing else, so a kind of place a history invents gets its locks and screens the way the presets do.

- **Locks follow `access`.** An `open` place locks nothing, whatever its rooms are called. An `admitted` or `private` place locks every service room its charter marks `shut`: `Door.locked` with `keyItemId` naming a `key` (a `keycard` in the `corporate`, `civic` and `industrial` finishes, which issue cards), and a `password` beside it where the place works at desks, because an office door has a keypad. A `private` place locks its street door too: a code on the door for a quest to hand out, and a `keycard` with `opens: { interiorId }` for the whole place. `src/interior/locks.ts`; the codes are a word and a number off `src/interior/codes.ts` (`kestrel-31`).
- **The key is in somebody's pocket, in front of the door.** Whoever keeps a place (`keeperOf` in `src/populate.ts`: the person at its counter, else on its door, else the first person with a job in it) carries every key and card the plan wrote for it, placed `at: 'npc'`, owned by them, named off the room it opens (`Cellar key`, `Evidence room card`, `<place> card`); their post is filled whatever the dice say, so a lock always has its keeper. The keeper stands outside the room they keep. One thing worth locking up lies in the shut room: the first of the place's stock goes behind the lock.
- **A gate of bars where the finish has them.** A locked inner door in a `civic`, `industrial` or `worn` place gets a `bars-door` piece standing across it, naming the door; a `domestic` or `corporate` one is a plain locked door.
- **A camera where a place keeps watch or admits people only so far.** `keepsWatch` in `src/interior/machines.ts`: `work` includes `watch`, or `access` is not `open`. It hangs in the corner of the entry room's back wall, 3.5 m up (`RoomPlan.mount`), `watches` that room, and claims no floor.
- **A screen wherever somebody sits at a desk or serves at a reception.** Every desk with a chair drawn up to it in a `desk-floor` or `private-office` carries a `terminal` facing the chair (`workstation` in `src/interior/furnish/pieces.ts`); a `waiting-room` or `lobby` counter carries one facing the room; a living room puts a desk in a free corner with the household's `laptop` on it and a resident at it (`work-desk` reads `resident` in a residential charter); every bar counter carries a `monitor` beside the till, the bar's game. Each stands `on` its host at the host's own top, like a till.
- **What a screen runs is decided once, per place.** `stampMachines`: a screen on a bar counter runs `snake` or `tetris`; the first screen in a watched place shows `camera-feed`; the first at a counter that keeps papers holds the `ledger`; the rest are `mail` or a `blank` desk. Every screen that is not a game is `locked` with a `password`, because people lock their screens and the code is what a quest hands out; a game is open to anybody. Machine ids are minted with the rest.
- **The narrator is briefed.** `InstanceRequest.has` is what the plan put in the place: `locked` rooms by name with how each opens (`key`, `card` or `code`), `machines` by room and program, `camera`, and `forSale`. Keys, cards and deeds are named here off what they open or own and are not in `things`. The offline narrator ignores the brief; `@gb/scribe` is handed it.

### A home for the player

- **Every city opens a home on purpose.** `homesFor(open)` in `src/interior/open.ts`: one, and one more for every eight places a brief asks to open, taken after the town's needs and the story's own place and before the ranking spreads the rest, because a home has little to offer a ranking and is what the player buys. A city of three places opens one home whatever its size; a brief asking for 24 opens four.
- **All but one are for sale.** `src/raise/homes.ts`: a city with one open home sells it; one briefed wide enough for two keeps one lived in and sells the rest. A home for sale is a place with an open door (`access: open`), `Interior.forSale` in whole credits (300 plus 15 a square metre of floor, storey by storey, moved 15% either way by the seed), and nobody in it. Its `deed` lies on the counter of a place that keeps papers (any counter when none opens), owned by whoever serves there, priced at `forSale`, named `Deed to <home>`; buying it over that counter is buying the home. A deed is never rolled as stock (`stockOf` leaves `deed` out of `papers`), only written by a sale.
- **Every other home is somebody's.** A residential place that opens and is not sold has people in it, and `Interior.owner` names the first one written; every resident carries `homePlotId`. Measured: one home sold at 2, 10, 20 and 32 blocks; a 5x5 town briefed for 10 places opens two, one sold and one lived in.

### Where fast travel boards

`stationsWanted(metres)` in `src/layout/stations.ts`: one station for every 500 m of the city's longest side, about ten blocks, rounded. It is a distance and not a share, so a city has a handful of entrances however many plots it holds and a town under 250 m across has none. The kind that boards (`transit: subway` in the charter, the shipped `station` preset) is never rolled by the mix (`src/theme/plot-mix.ts`), only placed: they go on seeded sites spread across the town, each the site furthest from every one already picked, before the rest of the town is rolled. Measured on one seed: 2 blocks (156 m) none, 10 blocks (626 m) one, 20 blocks (1,210 m) two, 32 blocks (1,916 m) four, 50 blocks (2,970 m) six, 116 blocks of 6 cells (3,768 m) eight. A share of the plots put 26 entrances in an 8x8 town and 157 in a 20x20.

### The quests

A town's work is written by recipes over the people and things it actually holds, never one template. What the seed and the theme decide:

- **How much work there is.** A density rather than a number: about one side job for every two people standing in the town, moved by how busy a town of this kind is and swung up to a third either way by the seed. The cast is a fixed handful, so a city's size has nothing to do with how much there is to do in it: measured on one seed, a 2-block town of 38 buildings and a 20-block city of 3,199 both come out with 9 quests. Two towns of the same size do not get the same number. The only ceiling is what the town can actually book: a couple of jobs per person who gives work, and one unclaimed thing per job.
- **How far a job sends you.** A job reaches into the part of town it starts in: the next street over, a few streets away, or about one in twenty, the far side of town. Those are metres, not a share of the map. With three places open, every errand is a walk between two of them, so a city's jobs are longer walks than a town's and are paid for it; a brief that opens a dozen places gets the near errands back.
- **How much of it is offered, and behind how many doors.** What a fresh player meets is not a share of the quest list, it is how many of the doors they can walk into have somebody with something to say. Three quarters of the side work is on the board from the start; the rest of it, and most of the main line, is behind the ladder. Measured on a 10-block city: 9 jobs on offer at the start, work behind 67% of the doors that open, and the first one two doors in. A gate is only worth having when there are ungated people to walk in on.
- **Work is spread over the people who hand it out.** A place that has handed nothing out is asked before one that has, and nobody hands out more than two side jobs, so a city of three places puts its work over the dozen people in them rather than on one counter. The main line is the one thing that runs out of a single place on purpose.
- **A main line, and what it is for.** The main line is the way into the town's own argument, which is the one its history put at stake: each link says in its own summary what is behind it and which side the person handing it out is on. A town nobody wrote a history for still gets the line, and still has a social order for it to be about; what it lacks is anything for a choice to mean. One link, and another for every six people standing in the town, up to four, all out of the busiest staffed place, and each one finished raises a standing flag. Side work waits on those flags, so the first morning offers a fraction of the town and the rest of it opens as they earn their place.
- **The town's argument, and taking a side in it.** A town with three links or more in it has two counters that matter: the busiest one, and the furthest staffed place from it, so the two sides are in two parts of town. At a seeded link in the middle, the line hands the player one thing that both of them want and makes them pick. From there the ladder is climbed from whichever side they chose, from that side's own person, for that side's own pay, and the other side's links are never offered. A town too small for two sides does not fork, and its line is the one it always was.
- **What the town remembers.** A choice that moves a number leaves no history, so every choice leaves a mark as well, and a mark outlives the quest that set it. There are four kinds and each names somebody the town actually generated, so a town's history is a set of specific facts rather than one boolean: `sided:<place>` (you did what they wanted), `crossed:<place>` (you went against them, and they know), `owed:<person>` (they owe you one), `allied:<place>` (the side of the argument you came down on). Later quests read them: the branches of the main line are offered on `allied:`, and no side job is offered by somebody whose place the player has crossed.
- **Standing is per place, not per town.** The premise names the sides of a town's argument, but a side is an argument rather than an address, so a town's factions are its places. A job pays standing with the place it was for, and coming down on one side of a choice moves that place's standing up and the other's down. A town of a few hundred buildings carries a couple of dozen standings, which is what makes two playthroughs of one seed end up in different places rather than at different scores.
- **The recipes.** Fetch one thing from somewhere else; gather several of a kind and count them out; carry a parcel two people want and choose who gets it; walk somebody home; do a job with something else worth picking up on the way; hear about what else is in a building from the right person; lift something and beat the clock with it; put something somewhere it will not be found; two halves of one job in either order; get through a locked door (`key-run`: the keeper hands the key over, or somebody in the place gets it off them, or somebody nearby knows the code, then `unlock`, the thing behind it, and the walk back; finishing it pays the run of that door as `access`); read a locked screen (`hack-job`: somebody in the place sells the code, then `hack`, then report back); win a bet on a game screen (`high-score`: `beat-game` at a score, then collect); a shopping list (`shopping`: `buy` a few priced things over a counter and bring them back, the bill repaid on delivery, offered only to somebody with `money-at-least` the bill). Each one says whether the town can serve it and how likely it is here, so a town without anything worth stealing never writes a theft and a town with no lock never writes a key run.
- **A job through every lock, before the errands.** A locked door is rare and deliberate: the charter behind it admits people only so far, so the writer deals a `key-run` for every lock the town has before it deals the side work at random, and a town with a disco always has a job into its cellar. What is behind a lock is not stock for any other recipe, and a person standing behind one is not in the cast: nobody is sent to fetch a thing or find a person the player cannot reach without the key.
- **What the ladder pays beyond credits.** The top of the main line is paid for what it is: `hard` in a town too small to fork and `epic` in one that does (`Load.atLeast` in `src/quests/difficulty.ts` lifts the band, and the pay stays inside it), and it hands over what the town has to give: a car (one of `@gb/world`'s `CAR_MODELS`) where somewhere in town works at a bench and has a mechanic to hand one out, and the deed to a home for sale on the `epic` finale, booked so no other line promises it. The fork is `hard` and pays the car when the finale pays the home. A `key-run` pays `access` to its door where the band allows one. No side job pays a car or a home.
- **What it pays.** `rewardFor(difficulty)` from `@gb/quest`, with the difficulty read off the work: metres walked door to door (400 m is one point of it, about ten blocks and the span the stations are set at, so the bands spread over the walks a city of three places actually produces instead of bunching every errand at the top), steps, whether it is a theft, whether it is timed, whether somebody has to be kept safe, and how much has to be carried. Inside its band a job is paid for where it sits, so a fetch across a hundred blocks is not paid the same as one next door, and a step that pays on top of the reward is kept inside what the band allows.
- **Nobody is sent back to the person they are standing in front of.** A quest never opens with a `talk` step aimed at its own giver; the conversation that hands the job out is the conversation.
- **A job ends in front of whoever handed it out.** The last thing a quest asks for is the hand-in: the delivery back to the giver, or a word with them where there is nothing to hand over. A `complete` step resolves the moment it opens and is never on the board, so a job whose last real step leaves the player across town simply stops wherever they are standing. The one exception is a road taken to cross that person, which is what taking it means.
- **No fork can strand a player.** Every rung of the ladder raises the same `standing_n` on both branches, so side work never waits on a branch and the ladder is always climbable to the top. A rung is written all at once: if either side of it cannot be written, neither is, and the line stops there rather than leaving a branch with a gap in it. A fork does make the other branch's links unreachable, on purpose, and that is the only work a fork ever takes away: crossing a place shuts its own side work and opens the side work of whoever you sided with instead. Both branches of every fork are played to the end in the tests, and a whole town is played twice over from one seed to prove the two runs finish in different places.
- **Timers are game seconds, budgeted the way `@gb/quest` says.** `secondsFor` in `src/quests/pace.ts` gives a timed job 600 game seconds for every conversation (one model reply is 200 to 450 at 24 game seconds a real second), 3,000 for every walk or the metres at walking pace if that is longer, half again on top, and never under an hour (3,600) or over a day. A hurried escort with three walks and two conversations gets about four game hours, ten real minutes.
- **The harness plays a living town, and keeps its locks.** `tests/player.ts` charges every verb its game seconds and reports the clock after each, so a timer too short for its own job fails there the way it fails a player; an escort is credited by `companion-arrived`, the one event `@gb/quest` takes for it, and nothing else. `tests/city.ts` reads the world's locks: a line pointing at a person, a thing, a surface or a screen behind a locked door is out of reach until the player has opened every door on the way, `unlock` opens one only with its key in hand, its code known or access granted, `hack` opens a screen only with its code known, `play` reports the score the line asked for, and a thing on a counter with a price is bought (`bought` and `acquired`) when the player can pay and lifted when they cannot. A job that stops at a door the player cannot open is reported as `shut`, never credited. `tests/street.ts` sends a third of the town out walking the way `@gb/app` does (in roster order, never the last person out of a room), and `playEvery` plays each town three ways: everybody at their post, a third out with `questTargets` kept in, and a third out with nobody kept, giving the player the flags and the money a job requires. Measured over a 2x2, a 10x10 and a 20x20 offline town: 35 of 35 at their posts and 35 of 35 with the targets kept in; with nobody kept, 19 of 35, the other 16 sent to a room whose person is out walking. `pnpm run playable` prints all three.

### Growing a finished city

A city is generated, played, and later added to as an authored step. `extend` is that step, and it is two kinds of growth a caller may ask for together (`Growth`: `blocks`, `places`; a bare number is `blocks`).

- **A door that was painted on opens.** `places` is how many. A plot that was a sign and a wall gains its interior, its people, the things lying about in it, and the locks and screens its charter puts there, exactly as a place that opened at founding does. The matrix does not change: no plot goes up, no cell is repainted, and the streets are the streets anybody already played. The facades are ranked the way the town ranked its first three (`#facadeSites` feeds them into the same `openDoors`): what the place holds, how much floor is behind its door, how near the middle of town it stands, whether it is on an avenue, whether the town's own story is about that kind, and a walk away from every door already open. A place that is open is never in the pot, so nothing is opened twice.
- **An opened facade keeps its sign.** The name over the door is the one the street has always read; the writer names the people and the stock inside it and not the place. That is what lets somebody who played the base recognise the building they are now walking into.
- **New land is a district.** `blocks` puts buildings up on ground nothing has claimed, one at a time, and that batch opens its own doors among itself: the town's own number of places, kept mostly frontage the way a hamlet is (`placesOnNewLand`, at most three). So a growth of new land is a piece of town rather than scenery, and it never reaches back into the city it joined.
- **The base is left as it was.** Every street, interior, person, item and quest the city already had comes out byte for byte as it went in, the history included. The one field a growth writes on a base record is the door pointer a facade gains when it opens (`plot.interiorId`), which is what opening it means. Measured on one seed, a 4x4 town of 100 plots grown by `{ blocks: 12, places: 2 }`: 108 plots, 3 to 8 interiors, 11 to 38 people, 16 to 43 things, and every base plot, interior, person, item and placement identical.
- **A growth writes work.** `extendQuests` writes the side jobs the town has room for that it has not got: its own appetite for work (which follows its cast, so it grows when the growth opens doors) less the quests it already carries, and never nothing, because a pack nobody can do anything in is scenery. They are written over the whole city, so a pack's errand can send the player back through the base as well as into what just opened, and every one goes through `@gb/quest` against the grown world. Ids carry on from the last one handed out. No second main line: a town has one argument and it was settled when the town was founded. Measured on that same growth: 6 more quests, 0 rejected, and the harness finishes all 15 of the city's quests.
- **Ids and determinism.** Everything is minted off the counters in the file, so nothing a growth adds can collide with what the base has. The same base and the same growth give the same city and the same quests twice over, because the facades are ranked off the town's own door stream and every site draws from a stream forked on its own plot id.

### How big a city can be

Nothing here holds the number of blocks down. The ceiling is `@gb/world`'s
`MAX_GRID_SIDE`, read from it rather than written here, and the brief's block
limit is that bound expressed in blocks: how many of the smallest block the
planner cuts fit across it, which is 116 at 2,048 cells. Ask for more than the
grid holds at the block size you named and the brief is refused before a cell
is allocated, with the grid it would have needed in the message. That is why
116 blocks of 6 cells builds and 116 of the default size does not (it would
need a 3,659-cell grid): blocks are not a size, cells are, and a road takes
cells too. The default block size fits 64 blocks a side, so the 50x50 city the
owner asked for builds without naming a block at all; past 64 a brief has to
name a smaller one.

`blockCells` argument) with the offline narrator. Another seed moves the

Avenues are counted across and down together. At the default block a city runs
about 0.07 ms and 0.24 KB a building all the way up, because a building that
does not open is a footprint, an entrance and a name, and the places, the
people, the stock and the work are the same handful whatever the size. What
does not stay flat is the ground: a brief that names a small block buys many
more streets per building, and the last two rows are the price of that, up to
12.8 s at the widest brief there is. What runs out first is still the player. A
20x20 city is 1,210 m corner to corner, a fourteen-minute walk at 1.4 m/s, and
it is what a brief that names no size builds; 50x50 is 3 km, half an hour.
Somewhere past twenty blocks a side, a city stops being a place you cross on
foot and starts being a place you live in one part of, which is what the
stations are for.

### The town plan

Two seeds are two towns to walk, not one town with the buildings shuffled. What the seed decides, before a single building is placed:

- **Block sizes, per column and per row.** A nominal side of 15, 17, 19 or 20 cells, then each column band and each row band takes that give or take two. Blocks are rectangles of two of those numbers, so a town has wide blocks and narrow ones and the walk between two streets is never the same length twice.
- **Every block faces four ways.** A block is cut into a strip of building on each side with a yard in the middle, and the middle has to stay deep enough for a frontage or the east and west strips are dropped. Any size that would fail that is nudged up a cell, which is why doors end up on north-south streets as well as east-west ones.
- **A missing street.** On one axis at most, an inner street is sometimes left out and the two blocks either side become one long block. Never two in a row, so a town never grows a field in the middle.
- **An open block.** Up to one block in four, and never the only one, is left unbuilt as a paved plaza or a green park. Both are painted on the grid, so nothing builds there afterwards and `@gb/nav`, `@gb/crowd` and `@gb/scene` read them as ground people walk on.
- **The avenues.** One street band in every five is an avenue, and never the ring road round the edge of town, and never two with one street between them. They are picked as whole lines, so an avenue runs the length of the town without a break and the ordinary streets hang off it. The interior lines are cut into as many spans as the town has avenues and one is drawn from each span, which spreads them across the town wherever the seed puts them. An axis with an inner street always gets one, so even a hamlet has a main street; an axis whose inner street was left out has nothing to promote and gets none.
- **The roads out**, below: how many and which walls of the valley they leave through.

An avenue is not a wider street. It is where the town happens: the roadway is 14 m against a street's 10 m and carries four lanes instead of two, the road out of the valley leaves along one, buildings that face one are built a storey taller, and a door on one is ranked for opening as highly as a door in the middle of town. So a player crossing a town meets a handful of long, busy, tall roads with quiet side streets between them, rather than one grid at one size.

A brief that names `blockCells` or `exits` gets what it asks for; the jitter still varies blocks around the number it named, and the size check measures the jittered width, so a brief that is accepted always plans a grid the world will found.

At a crossing, the roadway runs right through in both directions and the pavement keeps a corner in each quarter. That falls out of the painting order: mountains, then every pavement band, then every roadway band on top, each at its own class's width. Paint one band at a time and the last band wins the cells they share, which is how pavement ended up lying across the middle of every north-south street. Where an avenue crosses a street the wider roadway wins, so the junction is as wide as the widest arm of it.

So a pavement ends at the kerb: crossing a street means stepping onto the roadway, whichever way you are walking, 10 m of it on a street, 14 m on an avenue and 18 m on the road out. Anything that keeps people off the road (`@gb/crowd` walks `sidewalk` and `park`) walks one block's ring and no further.

### The roads out

The city sits in a valley ringed with mountains, and `brief.exits` says how many roads leave it: one to four, picked from the seed when the brief leaves it out, through walls the seed picks.

- It is the widest road there is, 18 m of roadway, because everything leaving the valley goes down it and nothing fronts onto it. It leaves from one of the town's own street crossings, straight out along that crossing's centreline, and it leaves along an avenue wherever the town has one running that way: the spine carries on out of the valley. Which crossing is seeded, so the way out is not always the middle of town.
- It carries the roadway across the pavement ring, so a car meets a T junction rather than a kerb, and pedestrians get a crossing where the ring pavement meets it.
- A 4 m pavement runs each side of it through the mountains, kerbed against the roadway by the same drop as any other street.
- It is in the road graph: a node where the roadway leaves the map, joined to the crossing's own node by a segment of kind `exit` carrying four lanes. `@gb/traffic` can drive it and `@gb/scene` marks it, because both read the graph.
- The pavement stops one cell short of the map edge and the roadway runs on to it, so the road leaves the grid instead of ending in two kerbs in a field. `@gb/land` grades the corridor for 120 m past the boundary and the haze closes the view; paving past the boundary is land's ground, not this box's.

## How to modify this blackbox safely

The `Narrator` interface is the seam for a language model: implement it elsewhere and pass it in; `OfflineNarrator` stays as the offline default and the reference shape. `src/narrator/one-at-a-time.ts` writes a place whole out of the three single-place calls, and is both what `OfflineNarrator` does and what a narrator with no `writeInstances` gets asked.

Putting buildings up is three steps under `src/raise/`: `planned.ts` is the shapes, `plan.ts` decides everything arithmetic about the town with no awaits, `homes.ts` puts the homes on the market once the whole town is planned, and `assemble.ts` writes the answers into the world in planned order and names the keys and deeds off what they open and own. Ids are minted in the plan and in `assemble.ts` and nowhere else. Keeping them apart is what lets the whole town be asked about in one call: add an await to `plan.ts` and the fan-out is a loop again.

A growth runs the same three steps, once per kind of growth, and the only thing that tells them apart is `Chosen.standing`: a site with one is a building the city already has, so it keeps its number and its sign, `assemble.ts` writes the interior behind the door it already had instead of putting a plot up, and it is never asked for a sign. Everything else, the ranking included, cannot tell the difference.

What a theme means lives in `src/theme/`: `flavour.ts` reads the theme text, `traits.ts` reads a charter's axes as the things a theme has a view on and holds each flavour's lean on them, `plot-mix.ts` turns those into weights, the keystone and the staples, `words.ts` holds the vocabulary a town names itself from. `src/charters/` is what a declared charter becomes: `resolve.ts` merges it over the presets, writes its wall pieces off `@gb/kitbash`'s `RECIPES` row for its frontage and openness, runs the gate and reports what it drops, `look.ts` turns a voice into signage and a frontage into a tint and the tags a catalogue matches, `hash.ts` is the digest the interior probe is memoised on. `src/narrator/` is what a name and a person are written from: `name-pool.ts` deals the head words out, `vocabulary.ts` takes the town's story's own words for it, `places.ts` composes a sign out of a head and the charter's templates, `signs.ts` is the sign over a door the narrator is never asked about, `streets.ts` names every street in the town, `roster.ts` deals the people's names out, `knowledge.ts` is what people are like and what they know, `lives.ts` is their own life, `background.ts` is the codex the player earns of them, and `one-at-a-time.ts` writes a whole place out of the three single-place calls, every question at once. `src/prices.ts` is what a thing costs, `src/populate.ts` is who stands where, in which body, and what a charter's holdings put on the shelves, and `src/premise/wants.ts` is what the town's story asks of its rooms.

Quest writing is `src/quests/`: `marks.ts` holds the four things a town remembers and how a place becomes a faction, `line.ts` plans the main line, where it forks and what its top rungs pay, `demand.ts` says how much work the town has in it, `cast.ts` is the town as a writer uses it (who can give work, who will walk with you, what nothing else has claimed, which locks and screens a job can be written through, what the counters sell, which home is for sale, how far apart two doors are), `stock.ts` is its ledger of what no quest has taken, `reach.ts` decides how far a job may send the player and picks by sampling rather than scanning, `difficulty.ts` turns the work into a band and its pay, `pace.ts` turns walks and conversations into game seconds, `targets.ts` says who a quest is waiting on, `write.ts` writes the line rung by rung, a job through every lock, and then the side work (a growth passes `from`, which starts the ids past the base's and writes errands only), and `recipes/` holds one recipe per file behind `recipes/recipe.ts`. Nothing a recipe calls may walk the whole town: a thousand quests over three thousand places is a quadratic, which is why the ledger keeps counts and every pick is sampled. A new recipe is a new file, a new entry in `recipes/index.ts`, and a `weight` that returns zero when the town cannot serve it. A recipe that can put two named people in front of the player and make them pick sets `takesSides`, and only those may write the link that forks the line. Recipes hand back drafts; `write.ts` puts them through `questDraftContract` and `sealQuest`, and a draft the door refuses is handed on unsealed so the forge reports it rather than hiding it.

The layout is eight files under `src/layout/`: `bands.ts` is the grid arithmetic every other one reads, with one `RoadBand` per class of road and the span of a whole town; `avenues.ts` says how many spines an axis gets, which lines they are, and whether a cell stands on one; `plan.ts` decides the whole town from one `Rng` and touches nothing; `streets.ts` paints that plan onto the grid; `exits.ts` plans and paints the roads out; `roads.ts` builds the graph; `stations.ts` turns the town's span in metres into a number of stations and spreads their sites; and `lines.ts` reads the bands back off that graph for a town with no plan to hand. Deciding and painting are apart on purpose: the plan is the only place a street number comes from, and it can be read and measured without a world. A width is never written down here: `bands.ts` reads `METRICS.road` and everything else reads `bands.ts`.

The `streets` stream is forked off the root and drawn from only in `plan.ts`, and `avenues` is forked off that for where the spines run. Draw from either anywhere else, or add a draw before them, and every seed lays out a different town.

`tests/fixtures/sealed-city.json` is a city this box built and sealed before the streets were seeded, quests included. It proves an already-exported city still loads and still validates. It is never regenerated: regenerating it is deleting the only proof that old files still open.

`tests/fixtures/golden.json` is the other fixed point: three seeds crossed with three histories (the offline one, one written in the presets' words, one that declares a kind of place), each pinned to the hash of the city and the quests it builds. `tests/golden.test.ts` holds every build to it, so a preset transcription or a draw order that moves is caught rather than shipped; with no model at all it is the city the presets build. `pnpm run golden` re-pins it, which is only ever right after a change that means the city to move.

The history is `src/premise/`: `shape.ts` is what a narrator writes (the premise, plus the charters it declares), `history.ts` reads one into what a city is founded on, `check.ts` checks the charters one by one and salvages the premise against `@gb/world`'s contract (the shape itself lives there), `render.ts` is the few lines it reaches a prompt as, `write.ts` composes one from the seed, `places.ts` is the kinds of place an offline history can declare, and `wording.ts` is the words it is composed from. Those words live in `premise/trades.md` and `premise/turns.md` and are compiled into `src/premise/wording.generated.ts` by `tools/bundle-wording.ts`: edit the markdown and run `pnpm --filter @gb/forge run generate`, never the generated file. A new thing a town can live on or a new thing that can have happened to one is a new `##` block and nothing else; a new kind of place it declares is a charter in `places.ts` and a `declares` line. What a premise then moves is in three places and no others: `src/theme/plot-mix.ts` for the mix and the staples, `src/interior/open.ts` for which doors open, and `src/quests/line.ts` for what the main line is about.

Interiors live in `src/interior/`: `draw.ts` says what a charter has to offer and what a town needs, `budget.ts` is the city's own number of places against the majority-shut ceiling and the doors a growth's new land brings with it, `open.ts` picks which ones and how many homes, `recipes.ts` reads a charter's rooms into a programme, `rooms.ts` cuts them out of the shell, `doors.ts` hangs the doors, `locks.ts` locks the ones the charter says and writes their keys and their gates of bars, `codes.ts` is what a password looks like, `machines.ts` says what every screen runs and which places hang a camera, `room-plan.ts` is the only way furniture and anchors get placed (it holds the clearance and reachability tests, and puts a piece on a top or on a wall), `stance.ts` says where a body's root goes relative to what it is using, standing at a piece, sat at it, sitting on it or propped against a wall, and `furnish/` has one routine per room use, dispatched in `furnish/index.ts` off `room.use` and nothing else, each handed what the town wants of the room (`RoomWants`). A new kind of place is a charter, never code: its rooms name uses that exist, `roleFor` reads its service and work, `stockOf` its holdings, and the gate refuses one nobody can stand in. A new room use is a new routine and a new case in that switch, which will not compile without one. Prop sizes are `@gb/world`'s `PROP_SPECS`, read through `src/interior/props.ts`, which adds `SEAT_SPECS`, the back rest and the pad of every piece a body sits on. A dresser never passes its own clearance or its own seat offset: it names the kind of anchor and the piece, and `stance.ts` answers.

Every generated quest is played to the end in the tests the way a player plays one, and the number that comes out is the one worth quoting. `tests/verbs.ts` is the list of things a player can do in the running game, with the box that produces each one and what each costs on the clock; `tests/city.ts` is the city as the player finds it (what belongs to somebody, what a counter charges, what a key opens, the doors between the street and anything a line names); `tests/player.ts` reads the board (`objectives()`) and nothing else, works out what a line is asking from the fields it publishes, and does it with one of those verbs; `tests/street.ts` is who is out walking; `tests/histories.ts` is the histories a narrator might write, with a jail that admits people only so far and a private villa; `tests/playable.ts` counts how many quests come out finished, in a town at its posts, a town with its quest targets kept in, and a town with nobody kept. A step whose verb nobody has yet is reported as blocking the quest, never credited, and a step pointing at somebody who is out is reported as an empty room, so the figure counts only what somebody could really do. `pnpm run playable [seed] [sizes] [theme]` prints all three.

Run `pnpm --filter @gb/forge test`, and regenerate `schema/brief.json` and the premise wording with `pnpm --filter @gb/forge run generate` when the brief or the markdown behind the premise changes. `pnpm --filter @gb/forge run preview [seed]` prints a town; `pnpm --filter @gb/forge run plans [seed]` draws one interior per kind of place the city declares as a floor plan, which is the fastest way to see whether a change reads; `pnpm --filter @gb/forge run measure [seed] [sizes] [theme] [blockCells]` prints the table above for whatever sizes you name; `pnpm --filter @gb/forge run reach [blocks] [seeds]` says what a fresh player walks into: how much work is on the board before they have done anything, and how many doors they open before somebody gives them something; `pnpm --filter @gb/forge run playable [seed] [sizes] [theme]` plays every quest of every size through the verbs the game has, in a town at its posts and in one with a third of its people out, and prints how many of them a player can finish each way.
